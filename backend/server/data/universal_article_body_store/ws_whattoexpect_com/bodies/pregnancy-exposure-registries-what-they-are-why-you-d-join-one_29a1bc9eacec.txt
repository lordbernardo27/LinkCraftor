Pregnancy Exposure Registries: What They Are and Why They Matter

pregnancy exposure registries, yellow and red pills on a yellow background

If you take a prescription medication or get a vaccine during pregnancy, your experience could become valuable knowledge for other moms-to-be.

When you're pregnant, every decision can feel like a big one — especially when it comes to your health. If you were taking a medication for a condition like ADHD, depression , diabetes , or high blood pressure before you were expecting, you might be wondering: Is it safe to keep taking this while I'm pregnant?

That's where pregnancy exposure registries come in. These studies offer answers, reassurance, and a way to help other moms-to-be down the road.

What is a pregnancy exposure registry?

A pregnancy exposure registry is an observational study, meaning researchers follow real-life experiences of pregnant women who take certain prescription medications or vaccines without changing anything about their care. Moms-to-be are followed throughout pregnancy, and sometimes beyond, to understand how these medications or vaccines might affect both mama and baby.

Why are pregnancy exposure registries important?

Before any prescription drug is approved for use, it goes through a well-controlled clinical trial to ensure that it's safe and effective. When it comes to pregnant women, though, the situation is a bit different — they're usually excluded from clinical trials because researchers want to avoid any possible risk to a developing baby. That means there's often not much information out there about how a drug might affect pregnancy until people who are already expecting take it.

That's where pregnancy exposure registries come in. These studies step in to fill that knowledge gap.

What about the recent news on Tylenol?

You may have seen headlines about Tylenol (acetaminophen) use during pregnancy. In September 2025, the Trump administration advised pregnant women to avoid it, citing a possible link to autism. But leading medical groups like the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) and the Society for Maternal Fetal Medicine (SMFM) quickly pushed back — they said the evidence just isn't strong enough to draw that conclusion, and acetaminophen remains the safest option for treating fever and pain in pregnancy when used as directed.

Some studies have suggested there might be an association, while others have found no clear link at all. Translation: more research is needed before anyone knows for sure.

Pregnancy exposure registries aren't used to evaluate the safety of over-the-counter drugs like acetaminophen (researchers use other tools for that). Still, this debate is a perfect example of why pregnancy exposure registries matter: when we don't have solid safety data, moms and providers are left with a lot of unanswered questions. The more we learn together, the more confident and supported moms-to-be can feel when making decisions for themselves and their babies.

Why would someone join a pregnancy exposure registry?

There are lots of reasons why a pregnant woman may continue taking a drug during pregnancy — maybe she didn't know she was pregnant (half of pregnancies in the United States are unplanned , after all.) Or maybe her provider recommended she keep treating an ongoing condition.

Whatever the reason, every shared experience adds valuable insight. Over time, pregnancy exposure registries help experts build a clearer understanding of how certain medications impact pregnancy. The data help both providers and the moms-to-be they care for.

Could joining a pregnancy exposure registry help other pregnant women?

Absolutely. If you're taking a prescription medication during pregnancy, joining a registry could help moms like you make informed, confident choices in the future. Your experience matters, and sharing it could be a powerful way to pay it forward.
