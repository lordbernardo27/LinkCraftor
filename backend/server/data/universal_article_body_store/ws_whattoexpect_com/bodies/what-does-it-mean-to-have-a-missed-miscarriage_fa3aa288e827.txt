What Does It Mean to Have a Missed Miscarriage?

A missed miscarriage can happen without the usual warning signs, leaving many women unaware until a routine checkup. Here’s how it’s diagnosed, why it occurs, and what to expect physically and emotionally.

If you have a missed miscarriage, it means that you've had a miscarriage but haven't experienced any of the usual signs. Your fetus or embryo has stopped developing, but you haven't had cramping or bleeding because you haven't passed the fetal tissue yet. Many women don't realize what's happened until a routine prenatal appointment.

Here's what you need to know about how this type of pregnancy loss differs from other miscarriages, how it's diagnosed, and what to expect as you recover.

Key Takeaways

A missed miscarriage often has no warning signs. Also called a silent miscarriage, it occurs when the embryo or fetus has stopped developing but there’s no bleeding, cramping, or tissue loss — so it’s typically discovered during a routine prenatal appointment.

It’s usually caused by chromosomal abnormalities and is not preventable. Like other miscarriages, the most common cause is genetic issues in the fetus. It’s not something you caused or could have stopped.

Treatment and recovery vary, both physically and emotionally. Options include waiting for the miscarriage to happen naturally, using medication, or having a minor surgical procedure. Physical recovery is usually quick, but emotional healing can take longer and looks different for everyone.

What is a missed miscarriage?

A missed miscarriage, sometimes called a silent miscarriage, occurs when a fetus or embryo has died but no bleeding or loss of tissue has taken place. Like other miscarriages , missed miscarriages happen unexpectedly before 20 weeks of pregnancy.

Missed miscarriages are often discovered at routine prenatal appointments , when no fetal cardiac activity is detected even though there have been no typical miscarriage symptoms.

While an estimated 20% or more pregnancies end in miscarriage, missed miscarriages are relatively uncommon. Specific numbers are hard to come by, but one study estimates that missed miscarriages affect less than 3% of pregnancies.

"I just found out that I'm having a silent miscarriage at 10 weeks," says What to Expect Community mom amandafeb2022. "We were discussing NIPT and VBAC, and then five minutes later we were discussing pills and D&C."

"I had absolutely no clue," says Community mom kristanne24. "I was still having all the [pregnancy] symptoms. My baby stopped growing at 9 weeks, and when I went in for my 12-week ultrasound there was no heartbeat. I was so stunned."

What causes a missed miscarriage?

Missed miscarriages happen for the same reasons as other miscarriages. Most often, the fetus isn't developing as it should. About half of all miscarriages are linked to chromosomal abnormalities, such as an extra or missing chromosome.

Miscarriages are never your fault. There's no way to predict or prevent them.

What are the signs of a missed miscarriage?

Missed miscarriages usually don't come with obvious symptoms, which can make the diagnosis all the more shocking.

In some cases, you might notice that pregnancy symptoms suddenly fade. Occasionally, there may be light brown discharge.

But by definition, a missed miscarriage occurs without common miscarriage symptoms such as cramping, bleeding, or passing tissue. Many women only learn of the loss during a routine prenatal visit, when an ultrasound or Doppler check shows no fetal cardiac activity.

How is a missed miscarriage diagnosed?

Missed miscarriages are usually detected during a prenatal checkup. If cardiac activity isn't seen or heard during an ultrasound or while checking with a Doppler monitor, your health care provider may suspect a missed miscarriage. An ultrasound may also reveal an empty gestational sac .

Depending on how far along you are, additional tests may be needed to confirm the loss. Your practitioner may perform an ultrasound if one wasn't already done, check whether the cervix has dilated or thinned, and order blood tests to evaluate hCG levels , blood count, and Rh factor. If you're Rh-negative, you may receive a RhoGAM shot to prevent complications in a future pregnancy.

How is a missed miscarriage treated?

Because there's no bleeding or tissue loss, the uterus needs to be emptied in order for your body to recover, your menstrual cycle to resume, and for you to try to get pregnant again , if and when you decide you're ready. That can happen in one of three ways:

Expectant management. Some women opt to wait for the pregnancy to pass naturally. This can take days to weeks, during which time bleeding and cramping will occur. If tissue remains in the uterus after the pregnancy passes, surgery or medicine may be needed to remove it. Otherwise, the lingering tissue could cause an infection.

Medication. Medications such as mifepristone and misoprostol can stimulate the uterus to expel the pregnancy. Tissue is typically passed within 24 to 48 hours. Side effects may include cramping, bleeding, nausea, or diarrhea.

Surgery. Dilation and curettage (D&C ), is a minor procedure used to remove tissue. It may cause mild discomfort and light bleeding and carries a small risk of infection, but it can complete the process more quickly.

Deciding which course to choose is a personal decision best made with your practitioner. Ultimately, the right choice is the one that feels most comfortable to you.

"I'm currently doing expectant management and would prefer to pass the baby at home, but I also don't want to drag this out for weeks," says Community mom raisingwildthings916.

Some women find medication or a D&C emotionally easier because it shortens the process. A D&C may also allow for testing to help determine a cause.

"The most important thing is to understand your options so you can make the decision that will bring you the most peace and closure," says Community mom MagnificatMom, who has had three natural miscarriages.

How long does it take to recover from a silent miscarriage?

Once the pregnancy has fully passed, your body will recover from a miscarriage within a matter of weeks. But there's no one-size-fits-all timeline for dealing with the emotions of a pregnancy loss, and no right or wrong way to process and accept your loss.

Physical recovery

Your physical recovery will depend on how the missed miscarriage was treated.

Expectant management: It's normal to experience period-like bleeding and cramping for a few days, followed by spotting that can last up to four weeks.

Medication or surgery: Mild cramping and bleeding for a few days are the norm. You may also have nausea or diarrhea.

In all cases, you can expect to feel physically better within a few days, even with continued spotting. Avoid sex and inserting anything in your vagina (including tampons) for a full two weeks to reduce the risk of infection. Your period should return within four to six weeks.

Pay attention, too, for signs that your body isn't healing properly. While infections after a miscarriage or D&C are rare, they can become serious if left untreated. Call your practitioner if you experience heavy bleeding (soaking through more than two pads per hour for more than two hours), fever, chills, or severe pain.

Emotional recovery

Recovering from a miscarriage emotionally is never easy, and a missed miscarriage can be especially difficult to process because there were no warning signs. No matter when the loss occurs, your feelings are valid. Take the time you need to rest, care for yourself, and process your feelings in the way that works best for you.

"Honestly, the fact that I had no idea it was happening before the sonogram that was supposed to be looking for the baby and heartbeat was what made the whole situation so sad for me," says Community mom Mamaofsooomanygirls.

Talking with a partner, family member, friend, or support group can help you make sense of what happened and find a way to move forward. But if there are times when you'd rather just be alone with your thoughts, that's okay, too.

You won't forget your lost pregnancy, but with time, you should gradually start to feel more like yourself. If that's not the case and you find yourself feeling isolated, withdrawn, anxious, or unable to cope with everyday life, talk with a mental health professional. Counseling can provide tools to help you move forward.

When can you try to get pregnant again after a missed miscarriage?

When to try again is a personal decision. You'll want to get the green light from your practitioner first, but most experts agree it's safe to try to conceive after you've had at least one normal period. It's okay to wait longer if you don't feel ready.

When you do start trying, you might worry about your ability to get pregnant again, and once you do get pregnant, whether you'll experience another loss. While these fears are normal, keep in mind that most women who have miscarried go on to have healthy pregnancies.

Community mom brittanylanedeal conceived again shortly after her D&C and now has a healthy 3-year-old. "My second pregnancy had no complications," she reports.

Missed miscarriages can be shocking and deeply painful. While you'll always remember the loss, with time and support, many women find a way to heal and move forward.
