Are Pregnancy Conditions Like Morning Sickness or Premature Labor Hereditary?

Light copy updates, updates to sourcing, and fact-checked for accuracy.

You may have your mother's brown eyes or her pretty smile, but will you have the same kind of pregnancy she had? You just might.

Your first pregnancy is full of unknowns (hey, even your second pregnancy can be a bit of a mystery), which is why you’re eager for info on what to expect. So when your mother-in-law mentions that huge babies run in her family or your own mom says she had a bad bout of morning sickness, of course you’re wondering if you’re in for those same pregnancy issues.

From long labors to gestational diabetes, here are some common pregnancy and labor conditions that may have a hereditary component.

Key Takeaways

Family history matters when you're expecting. Certain pregnancy experiences — like morning sickness, gestational diabetes, and even baby size — can have hereditary links.

Genetics can influence your pregnancy. Conditions such as overdue or premature labor may run in families, though genetics aren’t the only factor.

Your body type can play a role too. While long labors themselves aren’t inherited, physical traits like pelvic size can influence how your delivery unfolds.

Morning sickness

If your mother or sister had morning sickness, you might want to start stocking up on saltines and ginger ale now. Research has found a strong genetic link to this nauseating rite of passage, especially in severe cases. Even if you are predisposed to pregnancy queasiness, you can still combat it by always keeping some food in your stomach, sticking to foods that appeal to you and are easily digestible, and talking to your doctor about taking B6 supplements, which can nix nausea.

If you two were both on the smaller side as newborns (and are still petite people), then you probably won’t have a 10-pounder. If one of you was big and the other small, it’s anyone’s guess.

When you’re calculating, don’t forget to take into account that if you’re heavier as an expecting mom today than your own mom was back then, that may suggest you’ll have a bigger baby than she did.

An overdue baby

If you arrived two weeks after your due date , chances are your baby is going to be fashionably late too. Even so, you may not still be pregnant at 42 (or 43) weeks.

Here’s why: Ultrasounds a generation ago weren’t as common or as accurate as they are now, so due dates weren’t calculated as precisely. Translation: Your mom may not have been truly overdue .

Premature labor

A few studies have shown that preterm labor runs in families, but the research is very preliminary. What does that mean for you? If one of your family members went into labor early, tell your practitioner. Your doc will want to know what may have caused the early labor — so they can be proactive and protect you and your baby — and periodically measure the length of your cervix , which can be a tip-off about your risk of preterm labor.

A long labor

Although you can’t actually inherit these kinds of labors, your mom may have passed down her body type and that can affect what happens in the delivery room.

For instance, a mom with a smaller pelvis may have a long labor that ends in a C-section (because she’s too petite for a vaginal birth). Of course, plenty of other factors influence what type of labor you’ll end up having, but if you’re shaped in a similar way, it may affect how you give birth.

Just keep in mind that every mom-to-be — and every pregnancy — is different, and there's no guarantee that you'll have all the conditions your mom did.
