TITLE
The most uncomfortable part of birth might come afterwards

URL
https://www.whattoexpect.com/pregnancy/labor-and-delivery/fundal-massage

ARTICLE BODY

Key Takeaways

A few hours after birth, your nurse may press on your abdomen to check that your uterus is contracting as it should. This is called a fundal massage. These contractions help close off blood vessels and lower the risk of postpartum hemorrhage. Deep breathing can make the massage more tolerable, and a heating pad may ease discomfort afterward.

What needs to happen to your uterus after birth

After you’ve delivered your baby and the placenta , your now-empty uterus has an important job to do: shrink back to its original, pre-pregnancy size. As this happens, you may feel uterine contractions or uterus involution. These can be “intense and often painful during the first week following delivery,” says Sherry Ross , M.D., an OB/GYN and author of "She-ology." Uterine contractions help your uterus return to its original state, and also squeeze the blood vessels that supplied the placenta, explains Christopher Douglas , M.D., an assistant clinical professor of obstetrics and gynecology at the David Geffen School of Medicine at UCLA. “This is the body’s natural way of preventing heavy bleeding, or postpartum hemorrhage ,” he says. Though rare, postpartum hemorrhage is serious — losing excessive amounts of blood after you’ve given birth is a leading cause of maternal mortality, according to the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG). To make sure your uterus is contracting as it should and your bleeding is normal, your postpartum recovery team will routinely massage and press on your uterus. Regardless of whether you’ve given birth vaginally or via C-section , your nurse will likely perform a fundal massage for the first time immediately following birth and will then check every 15 or 30 minutes before you're transferred to the recovery room, depending on how you're bleeding is going. After that, your postpartum nurse will check you one to two times over the course of their 12-hour shift, sometimes more if they are concerned about your bleeding. In addition to pushing on your stomach, your recovery team might choose to give you Pitocin , a synthetic version of the hormone oxytocin. (If this medication sounds familiar, it’s probably because Pitocin is also sometimes given to induce or speed up labor.) Pitocin can help the uterus clamp down more effectively and reduce the risk of heavy bleeding. However, “even with Pitocin, sometimes the uterus doesn’t contract as strongly as we’d like right away,” says Dr. Douglas — which is why it can still be important for your postpartum nurses to continue occasionally pressing down on your abdomen during their check-ins to help the uterus along. The fundal massages can be uncomfortable, but they don't last long Some moms in the What to Expect Community describe having their stomach pressed on as “a little uncomfortable” or “a horrible experience,” while others say it was “totally not a big deal” and “ I barely remember it .” The massage itself is usually brief: “Once patients get to my care area, I do one good rub, maybe five or 10 seconds to make sure the uterus is really firm and at the same time, checking its location,” says Kylie Pina , RN, IBCLC, a postpartum nurse and lactation consultant. “From there, we usually have a check every four to eight hours where all I’m doing is checking that it’s firm and in the same place.” Though the massage itself only takes “a few seconds,” if a mom is experiencing extra bleeding, “that’s when we do a bit more of a massage,” Pina adds. Many moms agree that even if it’s uncomfortable, the fundal massage is nothing compared to the Herculean efforts you’ve already been through “I definitely didn’t enjoy it, but out of any part of labor and delivery, it was not even close to being the most painful thing,” says Community mom StephanieRachel . Is there anything that can make the process more comfortable? Fundal massage can be unpleasant, but there are some steps you can take to ease the discomfort. Here’s what experts recommend: Take pain medication. Your provider will probably already recommend that you take over-the-counter pain medication to ease your postpartum aches and pains. But pain relievers can also help reduce the discomfort you might feel when your nurse comes in to press down on your stomach, says Dr. Ross. Both ibuprofen and acetaminophen are safe to take if you’re breastfeeding. Practice deep breathing. During the fundal massage, focus on your breath and think calm, relaxing thoughts, recommends Dr. Ross. “Deep breaths helped,” says What to Expect Community mom AVmama24. “Inhale before and deep breathe out when they’re pushing on you. Staring into that brand new face is a nice distraction!” Ease the pain with heat. Contractions can be uncomfortable even before anyone is pushing on your stomach, but warmth in the form of a heating pad or hot water bottle may help the pain feel more manageable. “You can use a heating pad to help ease the discomfort. My [contractions] were uncomfortable for about a week, maybe a week and a half,” says What to Expect Community mom Brewingup5 . Talk to your nurse. If you’re feeling anxious about the fundal massage, don’t hesitate to let your nurse know during your next check-in. “They can explain what’s happening, pause if needed, and provide pain control,” says Dr. Douglas. It can also help to remind yourself that everything your postpartum nurses do is in support of your recovery, and pushing on your stomach is one small part of the round-the-clock care they’re providing. And, while the risk of postpartum hemorrhage is thankfully low, this brief step could save your life. “With one of my births, when they did the fundal massage, I was bleeding too much and [doctors realized] some placenta had remained,” says What to Expect mom yeiter . “If they hadn’t done that, they probably wouldn’t have caught it. It is important.”
