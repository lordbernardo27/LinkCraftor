Everything I've Learned About Single Parenting Through Grief After My Husband's Death

nicole warner widow essay

This is what I wish everyone knew about how to help another family through the unthinkable.

On January 15, 2019, I went from being in a blissfully happy marriage with three kids under 6 to being a single mom in an instant. My husband Marcus was running on the treadmill and died suddenly from cardiac arrest due to hypertrophic cardiomyopathy — at the age of 32. It felt like my world was put into a snowglobe and shaken violently.

The shock and trauma of the first two weeks after my husband’s death were debilitating. My house was flooded with family and friends from near and far, wrapping me in hugs, offering condolences and reminiscing about the wonderful man I married. My front door was open more than it was closed, letting in deliveries of flowers and fruit baskets and gifts. I was overwhelmed with emotion, shaking as tears poured down my face. When I was feeling my most vulnerable and torn apart, I had never felt so loved.

Church members and neighbors shoveled my driveway, cared for my children when we all had the flu and babysat so I could go to appointments. Meals were delivered for months, and it was so helpful.

I was (and still am, 10 months later), dealing with repercussions from my husband’s death, spending countless hours having conversations with funeral homes, financial institutions, the government, lawyers, counselors, teachers and behavioral therapists. I have had to send out copies of my spouse's death certificate, a weekly punch in the gut, to get accounts updated. And I am now managing a house on my own, expected to care for my children, be the sole income provider, keep up with children’s activities and manage their grief. All by myself. Even with my community behind me, there’s so much I have to do on my own.

So, if you are a friend of someone who lost their spouse, how can you help?

Show up. Don’t wait for them to ask for help. Drop off house supplies, snacks for the kids (or a bottle of wine for the parent!) or gift cards for gas. Leave a note at the door just saying you are there. Some of the best gifts I received were a gift card to have my house cleaned and a trip to the spa. Offer to watch their kids, and be specific about your availability. Remember that it might be really hard for your friend to accept help, so be clear that you want to help.

Check in often, but don’t expect a response. Give them a call on significant dates (holidays, birthdays, anniversaries). Random check-ins are wonderful, too. Ask how they’re doing and what they’re struggling with. If they are comfortable with talking, let them. They lost the person they shared their life with, so a listening ear is so appreciated.

On Mother’s Day, when I should have been celebrated by my husband, a sweet friend surprised me at my house by blasting my favorite song and bringing me a thoughtful gift. Now, that is love.

Talk about their spouse. Don’t be afraid to use their name. Ask questions about them. Tell your friend stories and memories you have with them. It has been more healing to hear about my spouse than to have people pretend he didn’t exist. It makes me so happy when I see pictures or videos of my husband that friends and family share. It reminds me that he was important and left behind a legacy. It’s a reminder that I am not the only one who lost him.

Understand that there’s so much you don’t see. After my husband died, the kids were confused and acting out. They wouldn’t even look me in the eye for two weeks. I lost my husband and felt like I lost my babies, too. Having willing family and friends to pitch in and fill in where I couldn’t was very necessary. Not being able to console my own kids was my worst nightmare, but counseling has been immensely helpful for my now-6-year-old and me. My two youngest (ages 5 and 3) haven’t grieved yet, although we talk often and openly about Daddy and missing him. Their grief will come later when they can fully comprehend the loss.

Just love. Listen to your friend, and don’t offer advice unless it is specifically asked for. They are managing SO much, and their choices might not align with yours. There are a thousand thoughts that run through my mind every day, and I’m constantly trying to balance what is best for my children and myself.

If you are walking alongside someone who lost a spouse, you are in a place of honor. Some of my darkest days were brightened by the smallest gesture from a friend. A text, a card in the mail, a sweet gift. Healing happens more quickly with support, so continue to care for the widow or widower, months and years past their partner’s death, because that is when it feels the loneliest. While most days I feel “normal,” I continue to have this painful wound to tend to.

My best friend, the father of my children and my life partner, died, and that isn’t something you just get over. I will always love my husband, and I will always be grateful for my amazing village that carried me through the fire.
