Parenting styles

What's your parenting style? From authoritative and authoritarian to free range and permissive, here are the most common parenting styles — and what you should know about each approach.

Once you have a baby and start getting to know other families, you quickly learn one thing: Every parent and kid is unique — and there’s no one-size-fits-all approach to raising children.

Still, while no two families are exactly alike, experts have identified some common parenting “styles.” These parenting approaches are generally based on the amount of support parents provide and the amount of control they try to exert. And most have their pros and cons — though some are generally considered to be more beneficial for kids than others.

So what are the different parenting styles, and how do they affect kids? Here’s a basic overview, plus clues on which camp you might fall into.

Types of parenting styles

Authoritarian parenting

What it is: This is a strict approach to parenting. Parents set high expectations and firm rules but may not offer their kids much support or ask for their input. The goal of authoritarian parenting is usually obedience — parents have high expectations, and may enact strict punishment when rules aren’t followed. Trusted SourceNational Institutes of Health, National Library of MedicineTypes of Parenting Styles and Effects On ChildrenSee All Sources [1]

Trusted SourceNational Institutes of Health, National Library of MedicineTypes of Parenting Styles and Effects On ChildrenSee All Sources

What it looks like: Authoritarian parents might not be able to set strict rules for a baby, but they tend to create highly rigid feeding and sleeping schedules. These parents “do not allow for a lot of flexibility or spontaneity in the day,” saysKyle Monk, M.D., a board-certified pediatrician at Cedars-Sinai Medical Center in Los Angeles, California and member of theWhat to Expect Medical Review Board. “While this may help create a lot of predictability for the parents and babies, some don't feel that parents may be as nurturing and responsive to babies' early emotional needs when they cannot yet self soothe.”

How it might affect kids: Teaching kids to have respect for rules can be a good thing. And some experts say that due to the strictness of this parenting style, children of authoritarian parents tend to think about their actions before they do something, so they are less likely to make impulsive choices. Children of authoritarian parents tend to be high achieving academically and disciplined, well-behaved, independent and self motivated. But research shows that an overly rigid approach may also put pressure on children to be perfect and might make her prone to internalizing behaviors like feeling withdrawn or shy, as well as possibly having higher levels of aggression. Authoritative parenting

What it is: Authoritative parents aim to strike a balance between being firm but also warm and supportive. Instead of forcing children to follow rules just because, authoritative parents will discuss rules and expectations as a family. But they’re still clear on who’s in charge and will hold kids accountable when they don’t do what’s expected. When discipline is used, it's usually in the form of coaching or guiding natural and logicalconsequencesrather than punishment.

The authoritative parenting style is thought to be the best approach, as it combines a supportive, loving environment while also setting clear expectations for the child.

Dr. Kyle Monk, pediatrician at Cedars-Sinai Medical Center and member of the What to Expect Medical Review Board

What it looks like: Authoritative parents of babies might create feeding and sleeping schedules, but they’ll make adjustments as needed based on what seems to work best for their little one. As a child moves into toddlerhood, authoritative parents will have non-negotiable rules about her health and safety (like no biting or throwing toys) and enforce them in a way that’s logical and respectful. That might mean taking a toy away if your child throws it at you or telling her goodnight when it’s bedtime instead of reading another story.

How it might affect kids: Authoritative parenting is widely considered to be a healthy approach for kids. “The authoritative parenting style is thought to be the best approach, as it combines a supportive, loving environment while also setting clear expectations for the child,” says Dr. Monk. “Parents are able to implement structure and consequences but may explain their reasoning so that the child can learn from her mistakes.” Children of authoritative parents tend to be confident, cheerful and cooperative, as well as curious, self-reliant and goal-oriented, research shows, as well as have high self-esteem. Attachment parenting

What it is: Attachment parenting stems from attachment theory, or the belief that infants come hardwired with the need to be nurtured and stay physically close to their primary caregiver for the first few years of life. Trusted SourceAttachment Parenting InternationalInfants & ToddlersSee All Sources [4] Some experts see attachment parenting as a specific subset of authoritative parenting, but one that places extra emphasis on affection and physical touch.

Trusted SourceAttachment Parenting InternationalInfants & ToddlersSee All Sources

What it looks like: Attachment parenting most often applies during the baby and toddler years, when a child has a higher need to remain physically close to their primary caregiver. It tends to involve minimizing separation from your little one, as well as breastfeeding on demand, holding your baby often and responding to her cries immediately.

How it might affect kids: Like authoritative parenting, attachment parenting has been shown to help children cope with adversity and stress, so she has more of the tools she needs to deal with life’s challenges. Trusted SourceAmerican Psychological AssociationThe Benefits of Attachment Parenting for Infants and Children: A Behavioral Developmental ViewSee All Sources [5] The approach can be challenging for parents though, since there is a risk of adults not taking stock of their own needs because so much energy is continuously devoted to tending to their children. Some children of attachment parents may also have difficulty independently transitioning to settings where their parents are not present.

Permissive parenting

What it is: Permissive parents are warm and nurturing, but tend to be lax (or inconsistent) in the rules and discipline department. They usually act more like a friend than a role model, so kids tend to have a lot of freedom and aren’t always monitored closely. Children of permissive parents don’t tend to have many responsibilities or chores, and their days usually aren’t very structured.

What it looks like: Being permissive with a baby might mean not setting feeding or sleep schedules. Once a child reaches the toddler or preschooler stage, she may be allowed to snack whenever she wants instead of at specified times, not be asked to clean up her toys when she's done playing with them or go to bed when she feels like it instead of at a set bedtime. “[Permissive] parents may try to enforce rules are set expectations, but their children are usually able to do whatever they desire,” Dr. Monk says. “Their parents may be seen as more like friends than parents.”

How it might affect kids: Children of permissive parents tend to be free thinkers who aren’t afraid to speak their minds. That might mean more creativity, for example. But being raised in a household without many limits can have some downsides, as being told "no" at home is a way for children to adapt to the reality that boundaries exist in every part of their outside worlds. “These children may not fully complete tasks like their homework, or cleaning their room, as they are not held accountable to do so,” says Dr. Monk. Kids with permissive parents may also be impulsive and struggle with self-control. Trusted SourceAmerican Psychological AssociationParenting StylesSee All Sources [6]

Free-range parenting

What it is: In some ways, free-range parenting is similar to permissive parenting. Both approaches are fluid and have very few guidelines. The difference? Instead of letting kids run amok just because, free-range parents value teaching kids to become more independent. In short, it’s allowing your kid to do what you feel she’s capable of — not letting her do whatever she wants.

What it looks like: Free-range parenting might seem like it only works for older kids, but the concept can be applied to children of any age. It might mean letting babies and toddlers explore new environments without interrupting or helping them (as long as they’re safe, of course). A free-range parent might let their school-age child play out in the backyard mostly unsupervised.

How it might affect kids: Letting children be more independent can foster a sense of resilience, where they’re better able to handle challenges and setbacks. “This model might not be best for every family, but it does help children become independent and confident in their choices,” says Dr. Monk. Free-range parenting also seems to encourage creativity and strong problem-solving skills. The main downside? Not everyone has the same definition of “free-range.” You might have no problem with letting your 8-year-old walk to school unsupervised, but others might see it as dangerous or even neglectful. Depending on where you live, there might even be laws against letting kids below a certain age do certain things by themselves. If you choose this approach, “it’s important to be able to find a balance between allowing your child to learn from their choices, but also steering them in a direction to be a well adapted and successful adult,” says Dr. Monk.

Helicopter parenting

What it is: The term“helicopter parents”appeared in the 1969 book Between Parent and Teenager , and is today used to refer to overprotective parents who tend to help their kids too much.

What it looks like: Helicopter parents mean well — after all, we all want the best for our kids — but people with this parenting style tend to smother their children with their help and supervision. They may be intrusive, overprotective, and micromanaging, or take unnecessary steps to protect children from harm.

How it might affect kids: Because they help their kids with just everything — doing homework, for example, or making unnecessary decisions — children with helicopter parents may not learn how to be independent and do things for themselves. And some research has found that over-controlling parenting can cause children to think the world is a scary place.

KEY TAKEAWAYS

The main parenting styles are authoritarian, authoritative, attachment, permissive, free-range, helicopter and uninvolved.

Most parenting styles have some pros and cons, but authoritative parenting is widely considered to be a healthy approach for kids. This approach creates a supportive, loving environment while also setting clear expectations for the child.

Uninvolved parents, on the other hand, aren’t able to meet their kids’ emotional or physical needs. As a result, this approach can be very detrimental for children.

Every family is different, and there are countless ways to parent. Not all parents fit into just one parenting style; you might be a combination of two or even three of the approaches on this list.

Uninvolved parenting

What it is: Uninvolved parents aren’t able to meet their kids’ emotional or physical needs, and provide little support or supervision.

What it looks like: Uninvolved parenting can take a lot of different forms — but most of us would know it when we see it. With an infant it can mean not meeting her basic needs of food and sleep and shelter, and with a toddler it could mean not looking out for safety hazards, not filtering what they say around the child, not screening shows the child is exposed to and not vetting the individuals they allow around the child or to care for the child.

How it might affect kids: Almost every expert agrees that uninvolved parenting sets children up for serious problems. While kids of uninvolved parents may be resilient, research shows that they also tend to have low self-esteem, and they can have a hard time forming healthy, trusting relationships with others. One recent study comparing the four main parenting styles (authoritarian, authoritative, permissive and uninvolved) found that children of uninvolved parents were more likely to have behavioral issues at school. Of course, every family is different, and there are countless different ways to be a parent. Not all parents fit into just one parenting style, either; you might be a combination of two or even three of the approaches above.

What’s more, with the exception of uninvolved, most parenting styles offer some benefits. Figuring out what works for you and your child means taking both of your personalities and needs into account — and considering the values you want to emphasize. As long as your little one is safe and getting the support she needs to thrive, there’s no right or wrong way to parent.
