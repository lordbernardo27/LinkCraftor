The 8 Best Postnatal Vitamins Are Packed With Nutrients and Easy To Swallow

Best Postnatal Vitamins: Six different bottles of various postnatal vitamins including Ritual and Nature Made

QUICK SUMMARY

According to OB/GYNs and moms, the best postnatal vitamin is theNature Made Postnatalwith DHA because it packs all the nutrients new moms need in a small, easy-to-swallow pill.

with DHA because it packs all the nutrients new moms need in a small, easy-to-swallow pill.

I would have paid (almost!) anything for a pill to make me feel more like myself after childbirth. Although no magical postpartum pill exists, there are plenty of postnatal vitamins that promise to fortify your body with nutrients your diet may be missing after pregnancy. As a mom of four little ones, it feels like I’ve tried them all.

Yes, it’s technically possible to get all the essentials through a balanced diet rich in animal proteins, beans, and lentils. ButDavid Ilceski, M.D., a board-certified OB/GYN at UnityPoint Health in Fort Dodge, says nothing beats the convenience of a postnatal vitamin — especially if you aren’t eating the most consistently balanced diet in the weeks following childbirth.

“I’m a fan of postnatals,” agrees new mom and dietitianJessica Cording, R.D., also author ofThe Little Book of Game-Changers .“Your nutritional needs are higher to support the healing process. There’s a lot of recovery that needs to happen.” And, if you’re breastfeeding, it’s crucial to do what you can to take care of things on the nutritional front for both you and your baby, points outChristine Greves, M.D., a board-certified OB/GYN at the Winnie Palmer Hospital for Women and Babies in Orlando, Florida.

While it’s just fine to finish up yourprenatal vitamins, or return to vitamins you took pre-pregnancy, postnatals are designed to deliver specific nutrients new moms need to keep energy levels up and help ensure breast milk contains all the essential nutrients that exclusively breastfed newborns need.

How I Tested and Chose the Best Postnatal Vitamins

The best postnatal vitamin has to do it all: Provide the right nutrients (and the right amount of them) to support your body through the postpartum period, and be something you’ll willingly take daily. With so many options at the pharmacy, I narrowed down the eight best postnatal vitamins by:

  • Leaning heavily on my experience using postnatal vitamins after four pregnancies (I’m currently postpartum and using one of these supplements religiously, too.)
  • Consulting with Drs. Ilceski and Greves, as well as Cording andJill Purdie, M.D., a board-certified OB/GYN in Atlanta, Georgia to learn what nutrients are needed in a good postnatal vitamin.
  • Reading through postnatal vitamin reviews in the What to Expect Community message board, paying close attention to ones that were recommended for their tolerability and cost.

Each vitamin that made the final list also needed to meet the following criteria:

  • Certified by governing bodies: Since the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) doesn’t regulate supplements like postnatal vitamins, I only considered those that have been certified by theNational Sanitation Foundation (NSF)andUnited States Pharmacopeia (USP), indicating the vitamins are tested for quality and safety.
  • Easy to tolerate: Pill size, flavor and aftertaste can impact your willingness to consume a postnatal vitamin, making these important factors.
  • Cause minimal side effects: “Some people may tolerate certain vitamins better than others in regards to GI-related side effects, like nausea or constipation,” says Dr. Purdie.
  • Have a relatively affordable price tag: Rarely are new parents flush with extra funds. To ensure your dollars are well-spent, we considered the cost per supplement serving.

A Doctor’s Note on Postnatal Vitamins

Doctors recommend keeping a few things in mind when it comes to taking a postnatal vitamin:

  • You don’t have to take one of these. As we mentioned above, you can technically get all of your nutritional needs from your diet. But many new moms are busy, making good nutrition less of a priority during this time. A postnatal vitamin easily allows you to fill in those nutritional gaps.
  • You can finish up your prenatal vitamins. Dr. Greves points out that you can technically take your prenatal vitamins in the postpartum period (I’ve done this, too). But these are designed for pregnancy, and you may not be filling all of your nutritional gaps as well if you take these supplements during the postpartum period.
  • Talk to your doctor if you’re unsure. Many will recommend taking some kind of supplement during this period in your life, according to Dr. Greves. But they can help answer your questions about any individual needs you have, as well as suggest certain nutrients to focus on.

Essential Postnatal Vitamin Ingredients

When analyzing the ingredient label of postnatal vitamins, experts recommend looking out for the following.

  • Iron: Iron is an essential nutrient that is an important component of hemoglobin, a protein in red blood cells that helps carry oxygen around your body. Blood loss and anemia (a condition where your body has lower-than-usual red blood cells) is common after childbirth, making it crucial to make sure you get enough iron, Dr. Greves says. Iron is also important for breastfeeding moms who don’t eat animal products to ensure their breast milk provides ample stores — especially after your period returns, since iron is lost through menstruation.
  • Vitamin D : This nutrient plays a lot of roles in the body, including supporting your immune system and brain health, according to theNational Institutes of Health (NIH). “It also may help with mood,” Dr. Greves says. One more thing to consider: Vitamin D helps yourbody absorb calcium, to promote strong bones and support your bone mineral density.
  • Choline: Helpful for brain development, accordingresearch, choline is found in foods ranging from animal proteins to vegan favorites like nuts, seeds and beans. Because your body needs 550 milligrams daily while nursing, it’s best to pop a postnatal pill that fills in where food leaves off.
  • Omega-3 fatty acids (DHA and EPA): Supplementing with the docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) and eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) found in fish oil can help fortify your breastmilk with the healthy fats that support fetal brain development, according to a smallstudypublished in Current Research in Food Science. While some vegan formulas derive DHA from microalgae, others include fish oil.
  • Folic acid: Folic acid is the synthetic form of folate, whichresearchsuggests supports cell accelerated growth during infancy. Breastfeeding women need 500 mcg (0.5mg) of folic acid daily to ensure there’s an ample amount in their breastmilk. That’s less than is needed during pregnancy, but more than a generic multivitamin typically provides.
  • Iodine: Added to certain types of table salt, iodine is a trace mineral that supports both brain development and overall growth in breastfed infants, plus thyroid hormone production, according to theNIH. Nursing women need about 290 micrograms daily, with 150 micrograms of iodide, a form that’s easily absorbed, via supplementation.
  • Calcium: Getting too little calcium could impact your own bone density, especially during a long breastfeeding journey:Researchsuggests lactating women lose up to 1000 mg of calcium a day to breastmilk. As such, the RDA for lactating women is 1,200mg/day, some of which you’ll find in certain postnatal supplements to pad a diet low in dairy.

And, if you don’t eat animal products, you'll also need vitamins that are rich in:

  • Vitamin B12: Found in animal products like meat, fish, eggs, poultry and dairy, plus fortified breakfast cereals and nutritional yeast, B12 can protect against the neurological delays that can result from this nutrient’s deficiency, according to areviewin the Pan African Medical Journal.
  • Zinc: Found in meat, poultry, seafood, eggs, legumes, seeds, and whole grains,zincis a key nutrient for immune functioning as well as infant growth and weight gain. Becauseresearchshows that a nursing mother’s intake of zinc has no bearing on the zinc content of her milk, supplementing with the nutrient can help ward off low zinc levels to keep the immune system at the top of its game.

FAQ

How long should you take postnatal vitamins?

Most doctors recommend taking postnatal vitamins for the first Six months postpartum right through your last breastfeeding session, says Dr. Ilceski.

What's the difference between prenatal and postnatal vitamins?

Because nutritional needs shift after pregnancy, postnatal vitamins look a bit different than prenatals. The biggest difference? “[Prenatal vitamins] have an increased amount of folic acid and iron necessary for fetal development, but typically exceed the requirements needed for postpartum mothers,” says Dr. Ilceski.

Postnatal vitamins designed to support lactating mothers usually contain iodine and choline, two nutrients you need more of when you’re nursing. So although many OB-GYNs do condone taking prenatal vitamins postpartum as a matter of practicality — and because there’s no great research that shows postnatal vitamins are superior to prenatal, according to Dr. Purdie — it’s best to choose and stick to a postnatal formula.

Are postnatal vitamins necessary?

Women who are vegetarian or vegan should take postnatal supplements with vitamin B12, since the nutrient is only found in animal-based foods, according to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC).

Among nursing mothers,maternal vitamin deficienciescan impact quantities of both fat- and water-soluble vitamins in milk. In other words, if your diet comes up short on certain vitamins, your baby’s breastmilk will, too.

Are postnatal vitamins FSA-eligible?

Typically, only prenatal vitamins are FSA-eligible.

Do postnatal vitamins help with postpartum hair loss?

It’s a little complicated. Someresearchsuggests that certain vitamins like biotin (which are often found in postnatal vitamins) may help with hair regrowth, but postpartum hair loss is different. Postpartum hair loss is typically due to the natural hormonal changes that happen after birth, and Dr. Greves says that vitamins will not change that. “However, postpartum vitamins could help with the replenishment of hair,” she says.

Are prenatal gummies, capsules, or powder better when breastfeeding?

One supplement form isn’t considered better than the other when it comes to breastfeeding, according to Cording. “Choose a supplement from a reputable brand,” she says. “But whatever form makes the most sense to you is best.” She suggests thinking about your lifestyle, as well as convenience, when choosing a type.
