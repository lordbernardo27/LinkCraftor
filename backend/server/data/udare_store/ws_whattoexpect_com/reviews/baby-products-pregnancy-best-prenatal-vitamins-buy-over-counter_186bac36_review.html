Quick Summary

The best prenatal vitamin is theNature Made Prenatal Multivitamin Folic Acid + DHA, according to feedback from registered dietitians, OB/GYNs, and testers from the What to Expect user community and editors on staff. These vitamins stand out for containing DHA and folic acid, being easy to swallow, and gentle on the stomach.

containing DHA and folic acid, being easy to swallow, and gentle on the stomach.

Your body needs a whole lot of nutrients when growing a baby. That’s where a daily prenatal vitamin comes in. Of course, no supplement can replace a balanced,pregnancy-friendly diet. But it's tough to meet the nutrient demands of pregnancy through food alone, and a supplement can help fill in any gaps, saysNicole Rankins, M.D., an OB/GYN and site director of the OB Hospitalist Group in Norfolk, Virginia.

And no, your everyday multivitamin doesn’t quite cut it. “If comparing them side by side, you’d see that prenatal vitamins offer higher levels of many nutrients than a standard women’s multivitamin,” saysOlivia Small, R.D.N., a prenatal dietitian and certified lactation counselor at The Ohio State University Wexner Medical Center. For example, prenatals typically contain more iron and folic acid — two must-haves to support pregnancy.

Editor’s Note: Health experts, including theU.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention(CDC) andAmerican Congress of Obstetricians and Gynecologists(ACOG), recommend that anyone who is pregnant or trying to conceive take at least 400 micrograms (mcg) of folic acid daily. Folic acid — the synthetic form of folate — is the only type of vitamin B9 proven in large studies toreduce the risk of neural tube defects, which affect the brain and spine early in pregnancy.

Some prenatal vitamins contain folate instead of folic acid . While folate is an important nutrient (typically found in dark leafy greens and other vegetables), it hasn’t been studied to prevent neural tube defects in the same way. For that reason, we’ve noted in the product reviews below any prenatal that does not contain folic acid, so you can discuss adding a separate folic acid supplement with your healthcare provider.

Ingredients to Look for in Prenatal Vitamins

Folic acid(400 to 600 mcg): One of the B vitamins that's crucial to the formation of the neural tube, which becomes your baby's brain and spine. It's the only form of folate proven to reduce the risk ofneutral tube defects(NTDs), according to the CDC.

Vitamin A (750 to 770 mcg): "Vitamin A plays a role in the formation of the fetal eyes, ears, limbs, and heart," says Dr. Purdie.

Vitamin C(80 to 85 mg) and E (15 mg): "These two play an important role in fetal collagen production and supporting the mother's immune system," says Dr. Purdie. Large doses of vitamin C a day (over 2,000 mg)can cause symptoms like diarrhea, nausea, and vomiting, so don't take additional supplements outside of what's in your prenatal without consulting your doctor.

Vitamin D(15 mcg or 600 IU): Vitamin D is important in developing fetal bones, as well as aiding maternal bone health. "There are also emerging studies that suggest it aids in immunity and improves mood for the mother," says Dr. Purdie. If your doctor suspects you may not be getting enough Vitamin D, she may recommend an extra supplement.

Calcium(1,000 to 1,300 mg): All women ages 19 to 50need at least1,000 mg per dayof this crucial nutrient, which, in the case of pregnant women, helps build baby's bones and teeth.

Iron(27 mg): During pregnancy, the amount of iron you need in a day greatly increases. This is because your bodyneeds more iron in order to make more blood to supply oxygen to your baby. "Iron requirements in pregnancy increase, so supplementation is necessary to maintain adequate blood cell production," says Dr. Purdie. If, like many women, you becomeanemic during pregnancy,your doctor may recommend an iron supplement or a different prenatal vitamin with more iron.

Zinc (11 to 12 mg): This mineral supports your immune system and healthy cell division.

DHA : Regular prenatal vitamins may not contain DHA, an omega-3 fatty acid found in many types of fish thataid in baby's brain development, both before and after birth. If you're unable to get the recommended two to three servings (8 to 12 ounces) ofpregnancy-safe fatty fishper week, ask your doctor if you should take a supplement.

Other nutrients : You might also see several other additions on the label, such as copper,choline, thiamin, vitamin B12, magnesium, and ginger or vitamin B6 (both may help with morning sickness).

  • Thiamine : Aka thiamin or vitamin B1, thiamine is important for the development of the baby's nervous system, Dr. Chuang says.
  • Vitamin B12 and B6 : Necessary for producing red blood cells, as well as the development of baby’s brain and nervous system, along with the placenta, Dr. Chuang says. "This is more of a common deficiency that's discussed," Dr. Greves says. "We check it in people who have less energy and bowel problems." B6 also aids in the metabolism of protein and may help with nausea. It should also give you an energy boost.
  • Magnesium : Supports healthy muscle and nerve function, Dr. Chuang says. It also helps to regulate blood sugar levels. "Sometimes we recommend that women with migraines take magnesium to help prevent them," Dr. Greves says.
  • Probiotics : "Probiotics can help support a healthy gut microbiome, which is beneficial for both the mother and baby's digestive health," Dr. Chuang says.
  • Choline : Helpful in the fetus's brain development and preventing some birth defects, according toresearch, experts recommend 450 mg per day while pregnant.

FAQs

Why do I need to take a prenatal vitamin during pregnancy?

Health experts recommend taking prenatal vitamins before and during pregnancy because they help the body meet the increased need for certain nutrients to support a growing fetus. Prenatal vitamins also help fill in nutritional gaps, especially in those who are having trouble eating well due to pregnancy nausea and food aversions.

When should I start taking prenatal vitamins?

Start taking prenatal vitamins three months before you hope to conceive, suggests Dr. Rankins. Taking supplemental folic acid — a key component of good prenatal vitamins — before you get pregnantcan reduce the risk of neural tube defects, which affect the brain and spine.

But if you didn't start taking prenatals until you learned that you're pregnant, don't beat yourself up. "Just take them as soon as possible," says Dr. Rankins.

Just like you can take prenatals before pregnancy, you can also take them after delivery. Prenatal vitamins can actually help fill gaps in your diet and provide essential nutrients when you're breastfeeding, says Dr. Greves. You can talk to your healthcare provider about continuing to take a prenatal after you give birth to see what is best for you and for how long you should take it.

When is the best time to take prenatal vitamins?

Ultimately, the best time to take a prenatal vitamin is whenever you will remember to take it (though experts recommend you take it with food). So if your mornings are particularly hectic, you may want to wait until nighttime to take your prenatal. If you’re experiencing nausea during your pregnancy, you’ll also want to factor in the time of day when you’re the least queasy.

Are there any side effects to prenatal vitamins?

Prenatal vitamins can cause mild side effects, including:

  • Constipation : "The pregnancy hormone progesterone can causeconstipation, and then if you add an iron supplement, it can be tough," says Dr. Rankins. Drink plenty of water and make sure you're getting enough fiber in your diet. You can also ask your doctor about a stool softener to help keep things moving.
  • Nausea and vomiting : Iron can also make people feel queasy. To combat this, Dr. Rankins suggests taking prenatals with "easily digestible" foods such as crackers or applesauce. You may also need to take your vitamin at bedtime, switch brands or even experiment with different formulas to help relieve these symptoms.
  • Bright yellow urine : Large doses of B vitamins, like riboflavin, may give your urine a more colorful hue that's typically harmless, but always check with your doctor if you're concerned.

If you struggle with side effects, ask your doctor about prescription prenatal vitamins. "Both prescription and over-the-counter vitamins will have all the necessary nutrients, however, the formulation may be different — the prescription vitamin may have iron that causes less constipation, or it may include a stool softener to help with constipation or vitamin B6 to help with nausea," says Dr. Purdie.

What is the most effective prenatal vitamin?

Recommendations for pregnancy supplements can vary based on your needs. To find the right pregnancy supplement for you, it’s best to check in with your doctor. They should be able to assess your needs and make recommendations on key ingredients to look for in a supplement.
