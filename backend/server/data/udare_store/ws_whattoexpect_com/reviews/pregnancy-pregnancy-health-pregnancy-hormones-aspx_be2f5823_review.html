Pregnancy Hormones

Your pregnancy symptoms are driven by a powerful mix of hormones working behind the scenes. Here's how these chemicals shape everything from your baby's development to your mood, body, and more.

Much of what's happening to your body now that you're pregnant — both the good and the not-so-good — can be traced to hormones. The truth is, this potent cocktail of chemicals can cause everything from blemishes and bloating to pregnancy brain and mood swings. Of course, pregnancy hormones are also doing the incredible job of supporting your pregnancy and preparing for breastfeeding.

"Crying and being mad at my husband are the two things I'm good at these days," jokesWhat to Expect Communitymom bababy327.

Read on to learn which hormones are most important and what roles they play in your pregnancy.

Key Takeaways

  • Pregnancy hormones drive nearly every change in your body. From supporting implantation and growing your uterus to developing your baby's organs and preparing your breasts for milk production, key hormones like estrogen, progesterone, and hCG make pregnancy possible.
  • They're also behind many common (and frustrating) symptoms. Hormones can trigger everything from bloating, skin changes, and congestion, to mood swings, brain fog, and digestive issues — the not-so-fun side of pregnancy.
  • Hormonal shifts continue through birth and beyond. Hormones like oxytocin and prolactin play major roles in labor, delivery, and breastfeeding, while a postpartum hormone drop can lead to emotional ups and downs like the "baby blues."

Here's a look at what each of these hormones does.

What is estrogen?

Estrogen is the main female hormone responsible for sexual development, including breast growth, and it kickstarts and regulates your menstrual cycle. It also helps to keep your bones strong and cholesterol levels in check. Trusted SourceHormone Health Network, Endocrine SocietyReproductive HormonesSee All Sources [2]

Trusted SourceHormone Health Network, Endocrine SocietyReproductive HormonesSee All Sources

Along with progesterone, estrogen is one of the two main hormones that get the pregnancy party started. Produced by the ovaries and later by theplacenta, estrogen helps the uterus grow, maintains the uterine lining, regulates other key hormones, and supports the development of your baby's organs. When it's time to breastfeed, estrogen also promotes the growth of breast tissue and supports milk flow.

Got a stuffy nose — or blotchy skin? Estrogen contributes to swollen mucous membranes and increases blood flow to your skin, which can result in redness or itchiness. It also plays a role in hyperpigmentation, causing darker nipples and brown patches (melasma) on the face. What is progesterone?

Progesterone, produced mainly in the ovaries after ovulation, works alongside estrogen as one of the "big two" pregnancy hormones. It helps prepare the uterine lining forimplantationof a fertilized egg and supports early pregnancy.

Along with the hormone relaxin, progesterone can slow digestion, leading to symptoms likeheartburn, indigestion,constipation, andbloating. The combination of progesterone and relaxin also helps soften ligaments and cartilage and loosens your joints to prepare you for labor.

And if yourgums swelland start to bleed, yourskin breaks out, or you feel supersweaty, that's the handiwork of this hormone, too.

"My gums have gotten so irritated and sensitive to anything, including a simple bite from an apple," says Community member EliMarie97.

What is follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH)?

Follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), produced by the pituitary gland, is part of a cascade of hormones that's necessary to launch your pregnancy and is present before you even conceive.

It stimulates egg development in the ovaries, which increases estrogen production. Rising estrogen levels then signal the body to produce a surge of luteinizing hormone (LH), leading to ovulation and potentially pregnancy.

Interesting fact: Women who have fraternal twins tend to have higher levels of FSH.

What is luteinizing hormone (LH)?

This hormone, also produced by the pituitary gland, works in concert with FSH to orchestrate your menstrual cycle. LH levels rise just before ovulation, triggering the release of an egg from the ovary. After ovulation, the follicle forms the corpus luteum, which produces hormones like progesterone to support a potential pregnancy.

If fertilization doesn't occur, the corpus luteum breaks down, hormone levels drop, and your period begins. If pregnancy does occur, it continues producing hormones to support early development.

Your doctor may check your LH levels if you're having trouble conceiving, since imbalances can affect ovulation and may be linked to conditions like polycystic ovarian syndrome (PCOS). Trusted SourceMayo ClinicPolycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS)See All Sources [4]

What is human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG)?

Human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) is often called the pregnancy hormone because it's only produced during pregnancy. Trusted SourceHormone Health Network, Endocrine SocietyBrain HormonesSee All Sources [5]

Trusted SourceHormone Health Network, Endocrine SocietyBrain HormonesSee All Sources

This hormone ramps up the production of estrogen and progesterone, and it's the hormone detected byhome pregnancy tests. Early in pregnancy, hCG levels rise rapidly, doubling every couple of days before peaking betweenweek 7andweek 12, then declining at the start of yoursecond trimester.

This hormone also plays a role in suppressing the immune system, sometimes leaving you more vulnerable tocoldsandthe flu.

What is prolactin?

Prolactin is another hormone that's made by the pituitary gland, and it's essential for milk production. Trusted SourceAmerican Society for Reproductive MedicineWhat is Prolactin?See All Sources [6] Its main job is to prepare your breasts for breastfeeding and stimulate milk production after your baby is born.

Trusted SourceAmerican Society for Reproductive MedicineWhat is Prolactin?See All Sources

Prolactin also stimulates the adrenal glands, which can trigger new hair growth in unexpected places (such as on the belly and face), though this fuzz usually disappears around six monthspostpartum.

What is placental growth factor?

Placental growth factor helps stimulate blood vessel growth, which transports the increased blood volume needed to nourish and support a growing baby.

Low levels of this pregnancy hormone may cause blood vessels in the placenta to narrow instead of widen, which may be linked to complications such ashigh blood pressureandpreeclampsia. Fortunately, blood andurine testscan help monitor levels for early detection and treatment.

What is human placental lactogen (hPL)?

Human placental lactogen, sometimes called human chorionic somatomammotropin, is produced by the placenta and helps regulate your metabolism to support your baby's growth.

The "lact" part of the name hints at its role in milk production: It helps prepare your breasts for breastfeeding and supports the production ofcolostrum, the nutrient-rich early milk.

In some cases, hPL and placental growth factor may contribute to insulin resistance, which can lead togestational diabetes.

What is relaxin?

Relaxin plays a big role in a woman's reproductive process. Levels rise after ovulation and help prepare the uterine wall for pregnancy. If fertilization doesn't occur, levels drop until the next cycle.

If you do conceive, relaxin lives up to its name by helping relax your muscles, bones, ligaments, and joints in preparation for labor. It also softens and lengthens the cervix. These changes can sometimes make you feel off balance and wobbly as you walk (be careful!).

"I feel like certain joints are shifting wonky, and I'm more sore and out of alignment," says Community member brcarr.

"I tripped on my outdoor stairs and rolled my ankle!" says Community mom ElleBellebear. "I've never fallen like that before and now I'm super cautious."

What is oxytocin?

Oxytocin, produced by the hypothalamus and released by the pituitary gland, plays a key role inlabor and delivery.

Though present throughout your pregnancy, this muscle-contracting hormone is best known for stimulatinglabor contractions. If labor is slow to progress, you might receivePitocin, a synthetic version of oxytocin, to help move things along. After delivery, oxytocin helps shrink the uterus and move milk into the breasts.

Are there other pregnancy hormones?

Yes, there are even more hormones at work during pregnancy, including:

  • Erythropoietin: Regulates red blood cell production.
  • Calcitonin: Promotes bone formation.
  • Vasopressin: Regulates fluid balance. High levels can lead to water retention.
  • Thyroxine: Increases oxygen consumption, supports fetal growth, and helps develop the baby's central nervous system.
  • Insulin: Controls metabolism in both mother and baby.
  • Adrenocorticotropic hormone: Triggers adrenal glands to release hormones that contribute tostretch marksandswelling.
  • Cortisol: Aids in fetal lung maturation. In high concentrations, this stress hormone may interfere with progesterone and contribute to brain fog.
  • Endorphins: Reduce stress and pain. These natural feel-good hormones may help you cope with labor pain — and perhaps even forget it once you meet your sweet new baby.

Are there postpartum hormones?

Even after labor and delivery, your body is awash in hormones. While progesterone and estrogen drop quickly after your baby arrives, oxytocin (sometimes called the "mothering hormone") and prolactin remain elevated to supportbondingandbreastfeeding.

This roller coaster mix of postpartum hormones can be tough for some new moms, potentially leading to irritability, tears, and the baby blues. These feelings are common, affecting up to 80% of new mothers. If you feel sad or out of sorts after the birth of your baby, talk with your doctor.
