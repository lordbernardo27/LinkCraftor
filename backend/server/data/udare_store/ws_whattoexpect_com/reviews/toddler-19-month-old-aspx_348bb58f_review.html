19-Month-Old Child

Whether you’ve got a whirling dervish or a laid-back little one, your best bet is to give your child plenty of time and space to do his thing.

Your 19-Month-Old Child

Catch me if you can! Your toddler may be on the go from morning ‘til night, as his energy levels and desire to explore collide in a whirlwind of perpetual motion. Some toddlers prefer a slower, quieter pace, however — and that’s just as normal.

Whatever your child’s personality, your mission is to help foster it — by providing lots of opportunities for safe physical activity, both inside and out, and by supplying your tot with things like crayons, puzzles and books.

Here’s what else is on the horizon for your 19-month-old.

At a Glance

Sleeping basics

Toddlers need about 11 to 14 hours of sleep a day, including naptime. Around this stage, many tots have dropped the morning nap and transitioned to one afternoon nap, sleeping for about one-and-a-half to two-and-a-half hours.

Feeding basics

Foods that contain omega-3 fatty acids are good for your toddler’s brain health. Find them in seafood, if your tot will eat it, nut butters (like peanut butter) and whole grains.

Your 19-month-old child’s growth

Despite your toddler's seemingly small appetite, he is continuing to grow in weight and height. (See, he knows what he’s doing!) At 19 months, boys weigh about 20 to 31 pounds, and measure 30½ to 35 inches long. Girls weigh about 18 to 29½ pounds, and are about 30 to 34½ inches in length.

Surprising as this may be, it’s all the more reason to make thosemini-mealscount, nutritionally speaking. Aim to introduce a range of healthy foods: bananas, winter squash, halved chickpeas, tofu, ground turkey meatballs and so on.

Most 1-year-oldsaren’t adventurous eaters, but the more you can vary their diet, the better. And who knows — your toddler may turn up his nose at tomatoes right now, but gobble them up with glee in a few months.

One thing you shouldn’t count on: cleanliness. Toddlers are just as content to squish, toss and mash their food as they are to eat it (sometimes even more so). There’s no well-visit until your child turns 2, but chances are, your toddler is getting into places he shouldn’t — resulting in a few bumps and bruises along the way.

Treating burns

Toddlers are notoriously curious. Problem is, those wandering hands sometimes touch objects they shouldn’t — like your coffee cup or soup bowl — which can result in a burn. Some are more serious than others and not all of them warrant a call to the doctor. You should get in touch with your pediatrician if your child’s burn is red and painful for more than a few hours. Call the doctor immediately for burns that form blisters (which are typically second-degree burns) or are white and look charred (which are likely third-degree burns). Also contact your pediatrician for any burns that appear raw or large (the size of your little one’s hand or bigger), and for those on the face, hands, feet or genitals.

Strep throat in children

Strep throat isn’t common in toddlers, but if your child comes down with the infection, he probably won’t be able to communicate how he feels to you. In toddlers, strep throat symptoms include an incredibly sore, red throat (often with white spots on the back), fever, irritability, refusal to eat or drink, and sometimes even vomiting.

Treating toddler sprains

Toddlers are more likely to break a bone than suffer a sprain because at this age, the parts of their bones called growth plates are weaker than the muscles and tendons, making fractures more common. But every so often, they’ll trip over a sidewalk or twist their ankle going down a slide, resulting in a sprain. If you notice swelling or bruising, or if your toddler seems unable to walk, call your pediatrician or on-call doctor right away or go to your nearest urgent care or ER.
