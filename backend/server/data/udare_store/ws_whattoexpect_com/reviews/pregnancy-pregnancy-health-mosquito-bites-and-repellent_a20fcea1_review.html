Mosquito Bites During Pregnancy: Why You're a Magnet and How to Stay Protected

Updates to copy, information, sources, and formatting

Paper Craft Magnet and a Mosquito

Does it feel like mosquitoes love you more than ever? You’re not imagining it. Here’s why pregnancy makes you extra appealing to bugs — and the safe, smart ways to protect yourself and your baby.

If you're pregnant and wondering how to protect yourself and your baby from mosquito bites, the answer is simple: insect repellent. Not only is it safe to use certainbug spraysaccording to the instructions on the label, it's also a smart way to guard against mosquito-borne illnesses. Here's what you need to know about staying bite-free.

Key Takeaways

  • Yes, you're a mosquito magnet right now. Pregnant women exhale more carbon dioxide and run warmer, which makes mosquitoes extra attracted to you. It's not your imagination!
  • Bug spray is safe — and your best defense. EPA-registered insect repellents (like DEET, picaridin, IR35535, oil of lemon eucalyptus, and 2-undecanone) are safe to use during pregnancy when applied as directed. And they're the smartest way to stay protected against bites and mosquito-borne illnesses.
  • Double up on protection. Repellent on exposed skin is step one, but you can go further: wear long sleeves and pants, treat clothing (not skin) with permethrin, avoid peak mosquito hours, and sleep in screened or air-conditioned spaces. And if you're using sunscreen, apply it first — then repellent.

What attracts mosquitoes to pregnant women?

If mosquitoes seem to love snacking on you more than ever now that you're pregnant, it's not just your imagination: Pregnant women really do attract more mosquitoes than non-pregnant women.

Experts think it may be because the pesky bugs are drawn to carbon dioxide, and pregnant women tend to take more frequent breaths, exhaling 21% more air. That means you're releasing extra amounts of the gas, as well as moisture, that mosquitoes find so appealing.

Another reason mosquitoes make a beeline for expectant mothers? They're heat-seeking, and pregnant moms generally have slightly higher body temperatures, thanks to all that baby-making going on.

"I've always been very enticing to mosquitoes but even more so now that I'm pregnant," saysWhat to Expect Communitymom cantbelieveit2025. "I get so many more bites being pregnant," agrees Community member abbyjb.

Are mosquito bites more dangerous during pregnancy?

Sometimes. When Zika virus infects a pregnant woman, it can causemicrocephalyin their babies, a rare birth defect in which babies are born with unusually small heads, which may lead to developmental delays.

For that reason, experts advise talking with your doctor if you plan to travel while pregnant to areas that currently have Zika cases or have had cases in the past. The good news? As of 2022, there are no countries with ongoing Zika outbreaks.

What to know about zika and other mosquito-borne diseases

Fortunately, most of the time, all this added attention from mosquitoes is just an itchy nuisance. But in some parts of the world, mosquito bites can spread illness that might be dangerous to you and your baby.

Mosquitoes can carry diseases — including West Nile, chikungunya, dengue, malaria, and Zika viruses. (While there have been no cases of Zika contracted within the U.S. since 2019, Zika virus disease still exists in 92 countries and territories worldwide.)

Visit theWorld Health Organization's websitefor travel advice — including transmission risks, vaccination requirements and recommendations, and additional information.

Is it safe to use mosquito repellent while pregnant?

Yes. The best way to prevent mosquito bites and the diseases they spread is to use insect repellent.

Choose a mosquito spray that's registered with the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA). All of the following active ingredients are considered safe for pregnant andbreastfeedingwomen when used as directed:

  • DEET, the most effective type of spray against mosquitoes
  • Picaridin, also known as KBR 3023 and icaridin outside of the U.S.
  • IR3535
  • Oil of lemon eucalyptus (OLE) or para-menthane-diol (PMD)
  • 2-undecanone

The EPA offersa handy insect repellent toolto help you determine which spray is best, depending on what bugs you need to ward off and how long you'll be outside.

For extra protection, you can also spray your clothing with permethrin. (Don't apply permethrin directly to your skin.)

"Natural" repellents made from purified plant oils, such as citronella and cedar, may help ward off bugs. But experts aren't sure of their effectiveness, so don't rely on them in high-risk areas.

Tips for using insect repellent and sunscreen together

If you're headed outside during the day, skip products that combine sunscreen and repellent, since they're just not as effective as the individual products used separately.

Instead,apply pregnancy-safe sunscreenfirst, then insect repellent. Keep in mind you may need to reapply sunscreen more frequently, since SPF decreases when DEET-containing repellents are used.

How to prevent mosquito bites

If you live in an area with mosquitoes, take these steps to protect yourself:

  • Stay inside during peak mosquito hours , typically from dusk to dawn, when possible. But be aware that mosquitoes carrying chikungunya, dengue, and Zika tend to bite during the daytime. "I try to avoid being out at night and apply bug spray," says Community mom debbiedee.
  • Wear protective clothing , including long sleeves, pants, and socks. Take extra care to protect your ankles and the back of your neck, as studies have shown certain mosquitoes can target these areas."I'm slightly allergic so if I go out I always wear a long-sleeved hoodie and use plenty of repellent," says Community member darc3.
  • Opt for permethrin-treated clothing and gear (such as boots, pants, socks, and tents) when possible. You can treat items yourself by spraying the product directly on your clothing as instructed on the label.
  • Use insect repellent on exposed skin.
  • Sleep in screened or air-conditioned rooms.
  • Keep up with travel alerts and follow all recommendations.

While most mosquito bites are little more than itchy annoyances, taking simple precautions can help protect both you and your growing baby — and give you one less thing to worry about.
