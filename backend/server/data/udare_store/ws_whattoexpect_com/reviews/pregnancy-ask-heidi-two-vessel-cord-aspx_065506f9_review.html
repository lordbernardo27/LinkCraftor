Two-Vessel Umbilical Cord? What It Means For Your Pregnancy

Updates to copy, sources, and formatting.

two-vessel cord, U-shaped magnet conceptual

Two-vessel cord — also known as single umbilical artery (SUA) — occurs in under 1% of singleton pregnancies and 5% of twins and multiples pregnancies. Here’s what to know.

There's nothing like an ultrasound to get your heart racing with anticipation (there's a baby inside!) and at the same time get you worried about every possiblecomplication. So if your ultrasound technician informs you that you have a two-vessel umbilical cord, otherwise known as a single umbilical artery (SUA), your first reaction may be to freak out.

Rest assured, this is one of the most common differences to affect theumbilical cord, the supply-and-waste line that connects you with your baby. Fortunately, most two-vessel cords have a happy ending — and a healthy baby to show for them.

Key Takeaways

  • Most babies are perfectly fine with a two-vessel cord. While this umbilical cord variation can sound alarming (instead of one vein and two arteries, this version has one vein and one artery), most infants with it are born healthy.
  • Expect extra monitoring. Once the two-vessel cord is seen on ultrasound (around 18 to 22 weeks), you’ll have more screening and scans along the way.
  • If nothing else is found, the prognosis is very good. There’s a slight risk of birth defects and slowed growth with a two-vessel umbilical cord, but most babies are born a good size and pretty close to on time.

What is a two-vessel cord?

During your ultrasound, it may be hard to process the information that your baby has a two-vessel cord. Here’s a quick explainer. In a normal umbilical cord, there are three blood vessels — one vein (which brings nutrients and oxygen to the baby) and two arteries (which transport waste from the baby back to the placenta and the mother’s blood).

But in some cases, the umbilical cord contains only two blood vessels — one vein and one artery. This condition is called either a two-vessel cord or a single umbilical artery.

How common is a two-vessel cord?

Having a two-vessel cord is very rare. Here's a snapshot:

  • About 1/2 to 1% of women carrying a singleton pregnancy and 5% of women with multiple pregnancies will have a two-vessel cord.
  • About 20 to 30% of babies with a single umbilical artery have health problems, including heart, kidney, or digestion problems and genetic conditions. This means that most babies with a single umbilical artery have no health problems.
  • Girls are thought to be more often affected than boys, though at least one study found that the sex of the baby doesn't have an impact on the risk of the condition.
  • When a detailed ultrasound of the baby is normal, the chance that the baby has a genetic condition is very low.
  • There’s no evidence that a two-vessel cord is hereditary.

What causes a two-vessel cord?

Researchers don't quite know what causes a single artery umbilical cord, though they think it happens because the second artery disappears at some point during early pregnancy.

You’re thought to be more at risk if you:

  • Are Caucasian
  • Are over 40
  • Are carrying multiples
  • Have diabetes or high blood pressure
  • Smoke
  • Got pregnant with assisted reproductive technology

Just remember, you can have a two-vessel cord without any of these risk factors.

It’s normal to worry about ultrasounds and other scans, but theWhat to Expect apphas your back with expert info and support from a community of mamas in the same boat.

How is a two-vessel cord diagnosed?

There aren’t any signs or symptoms. A two-vessel cord diagnosis typically occurs during a routinesecond trimester ultrasound, usually done between 18 and 22 weeks of pregnancy.

Although one artery can certainly perform the job of two and sustain a perfectly healthy pregnancy, your practitioner will likely keep a particularly close eye on your baby's development. Your doctor may recommend further tests, including:

  • A detailed ultrasound of your baby
  • Screening tests or diagnostic tests like cell free DNA oramniocentesisto check for genetic conditions such as Down syndrome
  • An echocardiogram, a special ultrasound that checks your baby’s heart
  • Extra ultrasounds in the third trimester, to check on your baby’s growth

What risks does a two-vessel cord present?

A single umbilical artery is usually enough to maintain a healthy pregnancy, but there are some concerns, including:

  • Birth defects. There’s a slightly higher risk that your baby might have a problem with other organ systems such as the heart and kidneys, or chromosomal conditions like Down syndrome.
  • Pregnancy complications. There’s a possible chance for problems likelow birth weightorpreterm delivery. But on average, these babies weigh 6.5 pounds and arrive after 38 weeks of pregnancy.

If your doctor doesn’t find any other abnormalities, a two-vessel cord isn’t likely to impact your pregnancy. Your baby is most likely to be born completely healthy.

What to Expect Communityparent Aunttef learned her baby-on-board has this exact condition: “They told me the main concern would be the baby's growth, but he’s measuring in the 68th percentile, so she said that doesn’t seem to be a concern currently," she says. "I will go back for another growth scan at 28 weeks.”

After delivery, there is no use for these arteries. They simply shrivel up and waste away. So, all you need to do from here on out is to continue taking good care of yourself and enjoy your pregnancy, keeping it as worry-free as possible.
