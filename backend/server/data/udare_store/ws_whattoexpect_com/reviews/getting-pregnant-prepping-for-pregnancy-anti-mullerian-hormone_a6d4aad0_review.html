What AMH Levels Really Mean for Your Fertility

Updates to copy, information, sources, and formatting

anti-mullerian hormone, hands holding egg

Anti-mullerian hormone is often one of the first tests in a fertility workup. Here’s what it can — and can’t — tell you about your chances of getting pregnant.

One of the first steps in a fertility workup is measuring markers that help estimatehow many eggs you have. One of those markers is anti-mullerian hormone (AMH). What does AMH tell you about your fertility? Here’s what you need to know.

Key Takeaways

  • AMH helps estimate your ovarian reserve. Anti-mullerian hormone reflects how many eggs you may have left, but it doesn’t predict whether you can conceive naturally or guarantee fertility outcomes.
  • Low AMH doesn’t mean pregnancy is impossible. Many women with low AMH levels still get pregnant, either naturally or with fertility support. AMH is just one piece of a much bigger fertility picture.
  • AMH levels naturally decline with age and can’t be reliably increased . While supplements are often marketed to boost AMH or egg quality, there’s no strong scientific evidence they work. Age and overall health are the biggest factors influencing levels.

What is anti-mullerian hormone (AMH)?

Anti-mullerian hormone is a hormone secreted by the supportive cells surrounding the eggs in your ovaries. The more eggs you have, the more AMH your body will produce.

How do you test AMH?

Testing AMH is as simple as rolling up your sleeve for a blood test. Your doctor can test AMH at any point in your menstrual cycle, since the measurement remains relatively stable throughout your cycle.

What do AMH levels tell you about your fertility?

“Anti-mullerian hormone (AMH) is a vital test for checking ovarian reserve,” according to theAmerican Society for Reproductive Medicine. Ovarian reserve is the number of eggs in a woman’s ovaries. As women age, their ovarian reserve declines, and AMH levels decrease until menopause.

Having a low AMH level doesn’t mean that you won’t be able to get pregnant. It just suggests that you may have a bit less time to work with whentrying to conceive.AMH levels can also help guidefertility treatment options, but they don’t predict whether or not you’ll get pregnant on your own.

“Don’t give up!” saysWhat to Expect Communitymom SarahEM32, who had a low AMH at age 36. “Low AMH doesn’t mean you can’t get pregnant, it just might be more difficult than expected. Letrozole and timed intercourse is what helped me get pregnant.”

What’s a normal AMH level?

There are age-specific averages for AMH levels, but in general, an AMH level more than 1.0 nanograms per milliliter (ng/mL) is considered reassuring. What’s considered “normal,” however, depends largely on your age and individual circumstances.

What’s a low AMH level?

AMH levels under 1.0 ng/mL are usually considered low, suggesting a somewhat lower supply of eggs. AMH levels less than 0.1 ng/mL may indicate a woman is approaching menopause, though timing varies widely from person to person.

Can you still get pregnant with low AMH?

Yes! Many women with low level AMH levels get pregnant naturally. AMH is just one number among many that doctors consider during a fertility evaluation, and it doesn’t measure egg quality or guarantee fertility outcomes.

“My AMH was 0.2 and I got pregnant naturally in 3 months,” says What to Expect Community mom capecod515151. “I just turned 34, so it was incredibly low for my age. Don’t give up hope!”

Can you improve your egg quality or your anti-mullerian hormone level?

A number of supplements, like antioxidant coEnzyme Q-10 (CoQ1O) and DHEA, have been promoted as ways to boost ovarian reserve, but there’s no reliable science to back up those claims.

Some studies have suggested that there may be a relationship between adequate levels of vitamin D and a higher AMH level, but again, there’s no conclusive data confirming thatvitamin D supplementsincrease AMH levels or improves egg quality.

Still, it can’t hurt to make sure you don’t have a deficit. Talk with your healthcare provider about which supplements might be right for you.

Causes of low AMH

Factors that may contribute to low AMH levels include:

  • Age. This is the most common reason for low AMH. As you get older, your ovarian reserve — and your AMH levels — naturally decline.
  • Genetics. Your DNA may influence how quickly your egg supply decreases.
  • Certain medical conditions. Chronic conditions such as endometriosis, as well as some autoimmune disorders (including autoimmunethyroid disease), may be associated with lower ovarian reserve.
  • Ovarian surgery. Procedures for ovarian cysts orendometriosiscan sometimes reduce AMH levels.
  • Cancer treatment. A history of radiation or chemotherapy may lead to a low AMH and early menopause.

These tests won't tell the whole story with 100% accuracy, but — along with your age, health history, and other factors — they’ll help piece together a picture of your fertility.
