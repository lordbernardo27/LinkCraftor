Gaining More Weight Than Expected? Here’s How to Find Your Healthy Groove

Updates to copy, information, sources, and formatting

gaining too much weight during pregnancy

For a healthy pregnancy, it's a good idea to roughly follow weight gain guidelines. Here's why you'll want to try to stay on track and what to do if you're gaining more than what's recommended.

No need to panic if your practitioner tells you that you're pacing ahead of yourrecommended pregnancy weight gain. There are plenty of steps you can take to get back on track. Don't diet, but focus on eating nutrient-dense foods in appropriate amounts, and stay active (with your health care practitioner's approval).

Read on for more tips and information about pregnancy weight guidelines.

Key Takeaways

  • A little extra weight gain during pregnancy is common and usually not a major concern . Nearly half of pregnant women gain more than recommended, and slightly exceeding guidelines isn’t typically harmful. The main concern is consistently gaining well above the recommended range over time.
  • Healthy weight gain depends on your starting BMI and should be guided by your practitioner. Recommended weight gain varies based on pre-pregnancy BMI (for example, 25 to 35 pounds for those in the "normal" range). Your healthcare practitioner can give you a personalized target.
  • Focus on healthy habits — not dieting — to manage weight gain. You shouldn’t try to lose weight during pregnancy. Instead, prioritize nutrient-dense foods, limit empty calories, stay active (with approval), and follow your practitioner’s guidance to keep weight gain on track safely.

How much weight should I gain during pregnancy?

In general, women with a "normal" pre-pregnancy BMI (18.5 to 24.9) are advised to gain between 25 and 35 pounds. Those who are underweight may need to gain more, while those who are overweight or have a BMI above 30 are advised to gain less. While there's ongoing debate about using BMI to define “healthy” weight, it remains the standard used in research and by the National Academy of Medicine to set pregnancy weight gain guidelines. Still, it's important to remember that BMI isn't a perfect measure.

If you step on the scale and find you've gained more than expected, you're not alone. In fact, 48% of American moms gain too much weight during pregnancy. Keep in mind that gaining a little extra weight usually isn't a cause for concern. After all, weight gain is essential to support your baby's growth. Problems arise when weight gain significantly exceeds guidelines over the course of pregnancy.

Your practitioner is the best resource to assess your individual needs and recommend a healthy weight gain range throughout your pregnancy.

How do I get my pregnancy weight gain back on track?

If you’ve surpassed your target weight gain, you can still aim to stay within range for the rest of your pregnancy. These strategies can help:

  • Talk to your practitioner. Yourdoctorormidwifecan help you monitor your weight and may recommend working with a nutritionist.
  • Skip dieting. Trying to lose weight during pregnancy isn't safe. Your baby needs a steady supply of nutrients, especially in thesecondandthird trimesters. Avoid appetite-suppressing drinks or pills, which can be dangerous when you're expecting.
  • Cut empty calories. The emphasis is on empty — you still need to eatenough of the right kinds of caloriesto keep your baby growing. Focus onnutrient-dense foodsand make simple swaps — like low-fat milk instead of whole, fresh fruit instead of dried, or grilled chicken instead of fried.
  • Watch portion sizes. A true serving is often smaller than expected. For example, a serving of meat or poultry is about the size of a computer mouse, and a serving of cheese is about the size of a 9-volt battery.
  • Eat filling, lower-calorie foods. Choose vegetables (especially leafy greens), fruits with high-water content (like melon), lean proteins, and whole grains like oatmeal. Drink water instead of soda or juice.
  • Prioritize healthy fats. About 25% to 35% of all your daily calories should come from healthy fats, such as those found in olive oil, canola oil, peanut oil, safflower oil, sesame oil, avocados, nuts, seeds, and fatty fish like salmon. Limit saturated fats, and avoid trans fats found in processed and fried foods.
  • Curb late-night snacking. Try to eat balanced meals and snacks earlier in the day, and keep evening snacks small and nutritious if needed.
  • Skip sugary drinks. Soda is the obvious beverage to eliminate, but juices and smoothies can also be high in sugar and calories. Limit intake and check labels on prepared drinks .
  • Stay active. With your practitioner's okay, make regular exercise a part of your pregnancy . While joining a prenatal yoga or exercise class is a great way to get and stay motivated, small amounts of activity add up fast and all count toward your recommended 30 minutes of daily physical activity. So incorporate some movement into your everyday routines: Park a few blocks from your destination, and take the stairs instead of the elevator, for example. What are the risks of gaining too much weight during pregnancy?

Gaining more than the recommended weight during pregnancy puts you and your baby at risk for health problems both during pregnancy and after, including: Trusted SourceAmerican College of Obstetricians and GynecologistsObesity and PregnancySee All Sources [3]

  • Less accurate ultrasound results . If you'reoverweight during pregnancyand have too much body fat, your practitioner may have a harder time looking at your baby (and diagnosing any problems that might require treatment) during yourultrasound exams. That may mean you'll have longer exams and possibly more ultrasounds.
  • Increased physical discomfort. Let's face it, pregnancy isn't all that comfortable to begin with — and those discomforts tend to multiply with the pounds. Excess weight gain can result in or aggravate everything frombackachesandpelvic painto overallexhaustion,varicose veins,leg cramps,heartburn,hemorrhoids, and achy joints.
  • High blood pressure . Havinggestational hypertension, which is diagnosed in the second half of pregnancy, can lead to issues duringdelivery.
  • Preeclampsia . Gaining too much weight can up your risk ofpreeclampsia. In turn, that condition can lead to liver and kidney problems for you as well as increase your risk ofintrauterine growth restriction (IUGR),placental abruption, and other complications. If you gain more than three pounds in any one week in the second trimester, or if you gain more than two pounds in any week in the third trimester — especially if it doesn’t seem to be related to overeating or excessive intake of sodium — check with your practitioner, as it could be a sign of preeclampsia.
  • Gestational diabetes . Being overweight or gaining too much weight during pregnancy puts you atrisk of gestational diabetes.
  • A large baby. The heavier you are, the more likely it is that you'll give birth to an overlybig baby (macrosomia)at birth (which can also happen as a result of gestational diabetes). Macrosomia increases the odds that avaginal deliverywill require the use offorcepsorvacuum extractionand ups your chances of delivering byC-section. Larger babies are also at greater risk ofchildhood obesity.
  • Preterm labor. The higher your pre-pregnancy BMI and the more weight you gain during pregnancy, the more likely your baby is to beborn prematurely. Preterm birth raises a baby's risk of a number of health issues depending on how premature the baby is, including breathing difficulties, eating challenges and, sometimes,developmental delaysand learning problems later in life.
  • Difficulty losing weight postpartum and a higher long-term risk of obesity. If you gain too much weight, you may have a harder timelosing the weight after your baby is born. What’s more, women who gain excessively and don't lose the pounds in the six months to year after giving birth are at a higher risk of being overweight or even obese 10 years later, which comes with its own set of health challenges.
