The 10 Questions Experts Say Matter Most Before Deciding to Have Another Baby

Questions to ask yourself

These questions may help give you some clarity.

Whether you have one child or already fill a minivan, that “should we do it again?” question might bubble up sooner or later. And navigating the decision can feel complex.

I’m a mom of two boys, ages 3 and 5. I love our family as it is right now, but I have two siblings and always imagined myself with a family as big as the one I grew up in. Recently, I had a conversation with my best friend — also a mom of two — about whether either of us wanted a third. We’re both nearly 37, and if we’re going to have another, it feels like this is the moment to do it. We’re also both very much on the fence: “If I picture myself in five years, I have three,” my friend said. “But the thought of doing pregnancy andpostpartumagain is so daunting.”

The immediate impact of having another child — those late-night, exhausted feedings, thehigh cost of childcare, and the general overwhelm from all the logistics — can feel like a lot. But having another child can be incredibly rewarding, especially 5, 10, 15 years down the road. Think, built-in besties, larger holiday gatherings, and celebrating milestones with more family members by your side.

The decision to have another baby will probably never be black and white. But to help you navigate it, I reached out to eight different experts — from mental health professionals to financial planners — for their takes on the most essential questions you should ask yourself if you’ve been going back and forth.

These questions can help you sort through how ready (or not) you feel right now, but for many, the choice ultimately comes down to the joy they get from parenting and whether they feel like they have more love to give, although everyone's journey is different.

“The main reason parents should choose to have another,” saysEileen Kennedy-Moore, Ph.D., a child development psychologist in Princeton, New Jersey, “is because they want to fall in love, again, with a little person, in that exquisitely ferocious and miraculous way.”

1. What’s my ability to handle extreme stress or other mental health issues right now?

Ask yourself this because… “A new baby shifts every routine, relationship, and resource in the household,” saysLee Philips, Ed.D., a licensed psychotherapist and certified sex and couples therapist in New York City.

Why your answer matters: Some parents say that adding asecond(or third, or fourth) baby to their family was ultimately less stressful than bringing home their first — but that’s certainly not the case for everyone. A baby can add stress to your life and routine, even if you have a broad sense of what’s in store.

So if you're already navigating a lot of stress or struggling with amental health conditionat the moment, adding another child can exacerbate things since it comes with sleep deprivation, hormonal changes, and financial pressures, Philips says.

Your mental health is the foundation of your family’s well-being. You can’t pour from an empty cup, and your children benefit most from a parent who feels grounded, present, and whole.

Lee Philips, Ed.D., a licensed psychotherapist and certified sex and couples therapist

It’s important to flag that having an existing mental health disorder doesn't mean that pregnancy will automatically make it worse — but there is that chance. And if, say, you’re currently on anSSRI medication(or a selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor), you’ll need to make a decision about whether you’ll stay on the script or not. Being prepared to address any mental health issues that may come up during pregnancy is critical, saysVictoria Fashakin Thore, M.D., FACOG, an OB/GYN at WakeMed in Raleigh, North Carolina.

2. What support systems do I have in place that I could step up postpartum?

Ask yourself this because… Every postpartum experience is different, but this time around could be just as challenging — or even more so — than the last. Are you ready for that? It’s important to consider, saysLea Schupak, LMHC, PMH-C, a licensed psychotherapist focusing on perinatal mental health in New York City.

Why your answer matters: The second time around, you may notice that you get less community support because people assume "you've got this," says Phillips.

You now also have older kids in the mix — will you be able to make changes in your routine so that you are supported and take care of your mental health? “Your mental health is the foundation of your family’s well-being,” says Phillips. "You can’t pour from an empty cup, and your children benefit most from a parent who feels grounded, present, and whole.”

Will you be able to easily tee up the support you might need from your family, friends, local moms going through something similar, or paid help? These things are important to think about as you plan for what another postpartum period might look like.

3. If you’re in a relationship, how would it handle the pressure and responsibility of having another child?

Ask yourself this because… "Anothernewborn stagecan impact physical intimacy, shift your body image, and create competing needs for rest and connection all over again," says Phillips.

Why your answer matters: If the two of you have done this at least once before, you have some idea what to expect. But a new baby can challenge even the strongest relationship.

Check in on the state of your relationship right now, says Phillips, and ask yourself: “Do we feel connected, supported, and able to communicate openly, or are there unresolved conflicts that another child could make worse?”

Be honest with each other about how you both handled those early parenting responsibilities, too. “Were there resentments or imbalances related to childcare, work, or household chores?” says Phillips. “What would need to change to make things more balanced with another baby?” Lastly, ask yourself how you and your partner usually deal with stress and whether you'd need to make adjustments there as well.

Depending on how you answered these questions, are you and your partner willing to actively listen to one another, communicate your own needs and desires, and set aside some time for discussions around things like household chores, intimacy, and the day-to-day schedules?

“Without intentional communication and empathy, the partnership can begin to feel like a logistical team rather than an emotional one,” says Phillips — so talking through it all beforehand is key.

4. If you have a partner, what feels like the “right” size family to each of us?

Ask yourself this because… Your ideal family size can change multiple times as your family dynamic changes. But right now, if you want to have another, it’s important to be on the same page as your partner, says Phillips.

Why your answer matters: Sit down with your partner, and picture your life 30 years from now. Adding a baby to your family right now might feel like it would be hard to manage in this moment, but when looking down the road, does the idea of having another (adult) child sitting at the table for family gatherings make you feel happy? Or does envisioning your family in the future help you see that it feels complete as-is?

Actively choosing to have another child should stem from emotional readiness, curiosity, and love — not guilt or external pressure. “Wanting to’ sustains you in the sleepless nights and unpredictable years ahead,” says Phillips. “'Having to’ often depletes you.”

5. How would another child fit into my child care routine in terms of day care, school, or family support?

Ask yourself this because… Adding another baby changes a lot of the day-to-day logistics for any family, says Kelley Abrams, Ph.D., a family and childhood therapist and a member of the What to Expect Medical Review Board.

Why your answer matters: Do you need to make any changes for another baby to fit into the family? Think about your current child care situation — would you have to do different drop-offs, or to find a new option altogether? If your children participate in certain sports or activities, how would a new baby fit into the routine you already have? What if one of your kids is sick and can't go to daycare?

“The logistics and overall demands on parents increase with additional children,” says Abrams. "Thinking proactively about how your family can incorporate the additional logistical hurdles is important.”

If you’re in a two-parent household where both parents work, could one job become more flexible or switch to part-time to accommodate the extra parenting load? Do either of you work from home (or could you start) to help navigate things like pick-ups and after-school activities? Or do you have family or friends nearby who could help make up for a lack of flexibility at your own job?

6. What would my plan be for navigating my family’s work situation during the newborn period?

Ask yourself this because… You’ll want to understand your parental leave benefits — or lack thereof, saysCara Pollard, a parental career transition coach in Chicago. Can you figure out a game plan that gives you enough time and resources to fully recover from a pregnancy, and also to integrate a new baby into your family?

Why your answer matters: From a planning perspective, it’s important to understand exactly what your current state'sparental leavepolicy looks like. Your employer’s plan may add benefits to the mix, too. (And it may have changed since your last pregnancy, even if you’re in the same role.)

If you have a partner, you should look at their leave, as well, so you’ll know whether or not they can help out during the postpartum period, says Pollard.

And if you don't have leave benefits and still want to have another baby? Do you think there’s a way you could still make it work? Or would you consider looking for a new job? These are all important pieces of the puzzle.

7. If I'm currently working, how might having another baby affect my career progress?

Ask yourself this because…The frustrating truth is that having more children does sometimes slow career progress for women, Pollard says.

Why your answer matters: You may not have to take a step back at work just because you have another child. But this scenario is one that families may find themselves grappling with — it’s important to consider how you would feel if this happened to you.

Mothers are paid 84 cents per dollar compared to fathers, according to the latest data from the Institute for Women’s Policy Research. This doesn’t mean moms can’t continue working towards long-term career goals like a promotion, an exciting new role, or an advanced degree, but “we have to be realistic in knowing these aspects do exist and persist,” says Pollard.

8. How could I manage the added cost of baby expenses with my current budget?

Ask yourself this because… Expanding your family can require you to tighten your spending a bit, saysEryn Schultz, a certified financial planner and founder of Her Personal Finance.

Why your answer matters: You probably have a pretty good sense ofhow expensive babies can be, with everything from formula to gear to child care adding up fast. It can help to review the major expenses you’d need to think about in the first year, and whether you are comfortable affording them, says Schultz.

Beyond the sheer cost of the stuff and the care, also think about potential housing and car costs, too: Can youstay in your current homeshould you have another child, or might space become an issue in a few years? Is there a creative way you can make your current space work for a longer period of time?

Some families also find they need a bigger car when they have another baby — for example, if you’re considering a third, would you need to buy something new? Or is there a way to make three car seats work side-by-side (even if it means you have to swap out your existing car seats)?

9. How would another child affect my ability to save for long-term goals?

Ask yourself this because… Welcoming another baby can cause your savings to dwindle, says Schultz.

Why your answer matters: Having another baby can understandably make it harder for you to put money into a retirement account, save for your child’s education, or achieve other long-term goals — and that’s okay, if it’s something you are willing to adapt to.

The love, joy, and desire to grow your family can lead to a sense of fulfillment and long-term happiness.

Phyliss Nicole Taylor, M.D., PMH-C, a perinatal psychiatrist and member of the What to Expect Medical Review Board

You may have to cut back on your savings to cover a period of unpaid leave or to afford baby essentials. Ideally, you’ll be able to come up with a plan that will let you continue growing your income or reducing overall expenses so you can get back to saving for the future eventually, Schultz says.

Also, if you’ve envisioned helping your child pay for college, will you be able to afford to help with another tuition bill if you expand your family? How does that affect your retirement plans?

10. Why do you feel drawn to having another baby?

Ask yourself this because… "Understanding your motivation behind wanting another child will help you feel more confident about your decision to have another baby or not," says Phyliss Nicole Taylor, M.D., PMH-C, a perinatal psychiatrist and member of the What to Expect Medical Review Board.

Why your answer matters: Do you just like the idea of another baby or are you all in for the joyful lifetime commitment? "The love, joy, and desire to grow your family can lead to a sense of fulfillment and long-term happiness," says Dr. Taylor.

And if your heart feels a little wobbly as you sort through all of this, that’s perfectly okay. Big decisions about growing your family aren’t meant to be rushed and may not always be clear right away.

As you imagine the life you’re building — the giggles, the juggling, the cozy chaos, the quiet moments — you might start to feel a gentle tug in one direction. Trust that. Your family’s story is uniquely yours, and you get to turn each page at your own pace.
