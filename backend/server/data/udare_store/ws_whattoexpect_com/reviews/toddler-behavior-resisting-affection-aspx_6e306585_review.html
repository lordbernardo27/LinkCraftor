Toddler Behavior

It may sting, but don't take it personally when your toddler wipes off your kisses and hides from your hugs.

Your toddler used to adore cuddling up for a kiss and giving (and receiving) big bear hugs, but now she spurns your smooches and snuggles. What gives?

No, your little one doesn’t suddenly hate you, although it might seem that way sometimes during theterrible twos. Resisting affection is often a totally normal part of toddler development, or simply indicative of your little one’s personality and social style. So take a deep breath and relax! It’s generally pretty normal — here’s how to handle it in stride.

Key Takeaways

  • Toddlers often resist affection as a normal part of development, using it to assert independence or because their temperament (shy, sensitive, easily overstimulated) makes hugs or kisses uncomfortable — not because they love you any less.
  • Support your child by respecting her boundaries, offering affection on her terms, finding alternative ways to connect (play, smiles, high-fives), timing physical closeness when she's more receptive, and avoiding guilt or pressure from adults.
  • Seek guidance from a pediatrician if your child consistently avoids touch and shows other social or developmental concerns (limited eye contact, little social engagement, ongoing sensory issues) or if it affects bonding or daily interactions.

Why are toddlers unaffectionate?

If your once-cuddly cub now rejects your advances, don't worry. For young children, resisting physical affection is often a way of showing independence and asserting control. Why? Your toddler is now able to do things like walking, running, andclimbing. She might not want to cuddle all day — she wants to use her new skills to explore the world around her and better assert her interests and needs. Stopping for a hug and a kiss from you may interfere with her grand plans to dig in the sandbox or run in the backyard.

Your child’s unique temperament — her personality and emotional style — also plays a huge role in how she displays and receives affection beyond the toddler years. Characteristics like mood, sensory threshold (how much stimulation she can take in and respond to), adaptability (how well she adjusts to change), intensity (the energy with which she responds to situations), and other factors affect how your little one sees and engages with the world. Ashy child, for example, probably isn’t going to willingly offer up hugs or accept them from new friends or acquaintances. Achild who is very sensitiveto touch might find hugs or kisses constraining and thus might try to squirm away from them. All of this is normal, expected variation because like adults, kids have their own unique personalities and preferences.

"My daughter isn't much for affection," says What to Expect Community mom Heathernel93. "If she gets upset, she will come to me for a hug and cuddles. She is a busybody, always moving and playing, but very rarely wants to come just sit in your lap and give loves."

This might change a little over time, but generally, a kid who is very shy or not physically affectionate now won't suddenly switch to being gregarious and hug-happy. However, just because your little one isn’t a big hugger doesn’t mean that she's antisocial or cut off emotionally.

Physical touch is just one way to communicate affection — and affection itself is only one part of socialization. Sharing a fun moment together, smiling, and looking at each other are just a few of many other ways that people can connect.

If your kid isn’t into kisses but loves to bring toys over to play with you or smiles at you as she runs by on the playground, she’s still being affectionate.

Is it normal for toddlers to resist affection?

Some resistance to cuddles and smooches is normal, especially during the super-independent toddler years.

It's completely natural at this age for her to want to explore and do things herself rather than sit back on your lap — it’s a sign of healthy independence! But if your child never wants to be touched and seems physically and emotionally distant, check in with your pediatrician.

What to do if your toddler is unaffectionate

Try these tips if your little bear seems aloof.

  • Don’t take it personally. It can feel like rejection when your child doesn't want hugs or snuggle time. But try not to personalize it. Remind yourself that this is a normal part of being a toddler, and focus instead on learning your child's developing style so that you can connect in ways that work for both of you.
  • Time it right. A toddler who rejects hugs while playing may relish cuddling at bedtime, after naps or when she's feeling anxious.
  • Catch and release. As soon as your child signals she wants to break free from your hug, let her go (and tell other family members and friends to do the same!). If she knows she can get away, she may feel less hug-shy next time.
  • Meet your tot in the middle. Some children don't like big bear hugs, but are open to a quick squeeze of the shoulders or pat on the head. Others prefer giving high-fives or blowing kisses. Do what your child is comfortable with and she'll be more open to being affectionate ... on her terms.
  • Find other ways to connect. Think about what your child likes to do and make extra time for those activities, whether it’s playing pretend, running and jumping at the playground, or rolling down a hill of grass. Doing things together, even if hugs and kisses aren't involved, are still bonding experiences.
  • Avoid scolding or pouring on the guilt. Skip saying things like, "Don't you love me?" that may make her feel bad.
  • Brief other adults in advance. To avoid an uncomfortable interaction, tell potentially overbearing grownups before visits about your tot's boundaries. Saying something as simple as, "She doesn’t like tight hugs, but she loves blowing kisses or giving high-fives," can go a long way to resetting adults' expectations and keeping everyone happy.
  • Don't force it. The American Academy of Pediatrics reminds parents not to force affection, as it sends the wrong message to kids that it's okay for adults to demand physical contact. When to talk to the doctor

Again, some reluctance toward hugs, cuddles, and kisses is totally normal and expected at this age. But here are some questions to ponder if you're considering asking an expert's advice:

  • Does she just dislike physical affection, or does she struggle with other aspects of social engagement? Shying away from hugs and kisses might just mean that your kid is sensitive to touch or values extra independence right now (perfectly normal). But it’s worth talking to a doctor if she also doesn’t like to make eye contact, doesn’t communicate social interests or affection in other ways, and/or is more interested in objects than people.
  • Has this gone on consistently for several months? Has it just happened a few times or has she regularly avoided touch for several months? And is she consistently avoiding social contact with everyone or just certain people?
  • Has she shown other signs of behavioral, social, or developmental challenges, like fascination with objects, extreme sensory sensitivity, or communication challenges?
  • Is it affecting your relationship or ability to bond with your toddler? Even if your child is going through a normal developmental phase, you may feel challenged as a parent. If interactions always end in frustration, or you’re struggling to figure out your toddler's style, it’s worth seeking help from a child mental health specialist, behavioral health specialist, or occupational therapist who can help you find strategies to get more in sync with your little one. "It can be very discouraging when we want to display affection and our littles are either too busy or just seemingly elsewhere," says What to Expect Community parent JazzyOx.

Next, talk to your pediatrician, who can evaluate your child and recommend follow-ups with specialists or additional screenings. Sometimes, conditions likeautism spectrum disordercan affect children's communication and behavior styles. Depending on what's going on, a specialist can help ensure you and your child receive the support you need to better navigate the world (and each other).
