Is It Safe to Get Botox While Breastfeeding?

Botox

Or will it put your baby at risk?

I'm going to admit something — and I’m hoping to do it in a circle of non-judgment (we are all in this together, right?). While I was breastfeeding my second daughter, I gotBotox.

Primarily, I did it in my masseter muscle, the muscle used for chewing, to ease my TMJ and round-the-clock teeth grinding, but I also had a little sprinkled into my forehead and around my eyes to help me look refreshed. Yes, I know, it’s generally not recommended during pregnancy and a little taboo whilenursing— but I made sure to consult with my injector, a plastic surgeon, first, to get the okay.

And as we continue to share this safe space, I know I'm not the only mom who has at least considered Botox postpartum, even whilebreastfeeding.

When one What to Expect Community mom,kole2083, asked if anyone had gotten Botox while breastfeeding (she, too, was struggling with severe TMJ), a handful of moms chimed in that their healthcare providers signed off on their own journeys with Botox while nursing.

“The question of whether or not a patient can get Botox while breastfeeding does come up often,” sharesMarnie Nussbaum, M.D., a New York City-based dermatologist. “People have waited their whole pregnancy, and they are excited to feel like themselves again, especially because they are exhausted and want to have a little pick-me-up.”

Before you let anyone poke you with a needle, let’s deep dive into what we do know about the safety of Botox during breastfeeding.

Key Takeaways

  • After skipping out on Botox during pregnancy, many moms wonder when it’s safe to start again.
  • Technically, experts recommend waiting until you’ve finished breastfeeding — there just isn’t enough research to confirm whether Botox passes through breast milk.
  • If you’re considering Botox for medical reasons, like migraines or TMJ, talk with your provider about the potential risks and benefits.

What is Botox?

Much like Kleenex and Q-tips, Botox is a brand name that’s become synonymous with the product itself. The full name is Botox Cosmetic, and it refers to onabotulinumtoxinA, a type of neurotoxin, or medication that is injected into a muscle to temporarily block nerve signals, according to theCleveland Clinic. Basically, “it temporarily paralyzes or weakens the muscles,” says Dr. Nussbaum.

Neurotoxins aren’t just for smoothing out fine lines — they can also be used for medical reasons, like easing migraines or calming an overactive jaw. Here’s how they work: these tiny injections temporarily block certain muscle contractions (think the ones that make you furrow your brow or squint). When those muscles take a break, your skin looks smoother, softer, and more relaxed. If you have migraines, it helps block the release of the chemicals that tell your brain you're experiencing that pain.

Botox is done in a medical setting by a healthcare provider who uses a tiny needle to inject the neurotoxin into the targeted muscle. It feels like a quick pinch, and injecting an entire area usually takes just a few minutes — which is why it’s often called a “lunchtime treatment.” The amount injected is measured in units, and your provider will tailor the exact units to your muscle size, the Botox strength, and what your desired result of the treatment is.

While Botox sounds like a quick fix for many issues, it’s still a drug.

Botox is made from a bacterium that also occurs naturally in soil and honey, according to Jay Lovenheim, M.D., a pediatrician in New Jersey. But just because it’s “nature” doesn’t mean it’s “safe.” (Poison ivy is “natural,” for instance.)

Can Botox pass to your baby through breast milk?

"We don’t know for certain if any of the medication passes intobreast milk, or if it could affect your baby or milk supply," says Shannon Smith, M.D., an OB/GYN in Massachusetts and What to Expect Medical Review Board member.

There has been data that has shown trace amounts of Botox in breast milk; however, these levels are extremely low and far below the amount considered dangerous for newborns, she adds. The amount injected for TMJ, migraines, or cosmetic reasons would very likely not reach that mark, according to the National Institutes of Health.

So far, published reports and medical reviews haven’t found problems in babies exposed through breastfeeding, but again, the available data is limited, Dr. Smith explains.

Experts generally believe that Botox is very unlikely to get into breast milk in amounts that would matter, says Dr. Smith. "The medication is a large molecule, injected locally, and isn’t well absorbed into the bloodstream. "Medicines like this are considered low risk for breastfed babies," she adds.

However, if you are considering Botox while breastfeeding, it’s a good idea to talk through your personal situation with your doctor so you can make a decision that balances your needs with your baby’s safety.

“Because of the lack of studies done on Botox and nursing, most physicians would likely have you avoid the injections if they are not necessary out of an abundance of caution,” says Dr. Lovenheim.

Still, there are many people (like yours truly!) who received the treatment for medical needs. Ultimately, for some, the benefits outweigh the risks.

What is the risk of getting Botox while breastfeeding?

Botox contains a bacterium that could cause infant botulism, says Dr. Lovenheim. The condition typically occurs in babies under one when bacteria get into their intestines and produce a toxin that affects the nerves. This can cause muscle weakness or paralysis. This same bacterium can contaminatehoney, which is the reason it is not recommended for those under 1.

Since Botox is injected into muscles and not ingested by the gut (like honey would be), the risk of neurotoxins causing infant botulism is minimal, but the risk still exists.

For me, calming my TMJ helped me show up as a better parent since I wasn’t clenching my jaw, grinding away at my teeth, and sending tension to my neck. I felt better, and it allowed me to relax better during a time when sleep was hard to come by. If you’re doing it for aesthetic purposes solely, consider waiting until you’re done nursing and pumping.

What to know if you try Botox while breastfeeding

When it comes to Botox and breastfeeding, the answer isn’t completely clear-cut — that’s why it’s worth weighing your options carefully with your provider.

Right now, we know that the risk to your baby is likely very low, especially when injections are done correctly and for medical reasons. Still, most experts say if you can hold off, it’s the safer choice — simply because there’s not enough long-term data yet to say for sure. Ultimately, this is a personal decision best made alongside your healthcare provider and your baby’s pediatrician. "The answer won't be the same for everyone as we all have different levels of risk tolerance," says Dr. Smith.

If you’re considering going for it, here are a few things to keep in mind:

  • Pick your injector wisely. This is not the moment to chase a bargain. Look for a board-certified injector who’s trained in facial anatomy and the science of neurotoxins (so they know exactly where — and where not — to place those tiny needles). Treatments are typically spaced three to four months apart, with results showing up anywhere from a few days to about a month after your appointment, according toBotox Cosmetic.
  • Know the side effects. Most people sail through without any issues, but it’s possible to have temporary side effects like bruising, mild swelling at the injection site, or a light headache, says Dr. Nussbaum. The good news? These usually fade within a couple of days.
  • Time it right. If you decide to move forward, you can nurse just before your appointment, then wait a few hours before breastfeeding again to help minimize any possible exposure. Some parents also choose to “pump and dump” the feeding right after injections — not because the science demands it, but for extra peace of mind, says Dr. Lovenheim.

Bottom line: If you’re eager to freshen up your look while breastfeeding, the overall risk appears low, but the safest path is still waiting until you’re done nursing. And remember — feeling good about yourself, in whatever way that looks for you, is always a worthy goal.
