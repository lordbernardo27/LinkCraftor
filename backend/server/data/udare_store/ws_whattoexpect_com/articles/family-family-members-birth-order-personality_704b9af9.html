Are the 'Eldest Daughter' Stereotypes True? What Science Says

colored rocks in order

Birth order labels have charm, but experts say a bigger, richer picture shapes your little one’s temperament.

I joke that my 2-year-old daughter is a budding director. She tells people where to sit, what to eat, and what to wear. (And yes, the adults in her life usually comply, within reason.) A few people have called her “bossy.” She’s a stereotypical only child. And we’re her equally stereotypical hypervigilant parents.

Even Taylor Swift nods to her big-sister vibes in her ever-so-popular song "Eldest Daughter." That "never gonna let you down" pressure she felt growing up — to be strong, be steady, be everyone’s rock — still sneaks into her grown-up life like a familiar little shadow.

As I watch these birth order stereotypes play out in my home and in memes (and songs!) online, I wonder whether there’s any truth behind birth order theory.

“We like to put people in certain boxes so that we know how to deal with them, and we know what to expect,” saysMichelle Maidenberg, Ph.D., a child and family therapist in White Plains, New York.

Here's what the research really says, the common stereotypes to take with a grain of salt, and some simple ways to help your child’s individualities shine.

Key Takeaways

  • Old wives' tales claim that a child's birth order may significantly affect their temperament, personality, and how responsible (or irresponsible!) they are.
  • Research suggests that each child is unique, regardless of their birth order.
  • Giving age-appropriate responsibilities and avoiding fixed sibling “roles” can build confidence and help each child grow into their own strengths.

Does birth order affect your child’s personality?

There’s a bit of truth to the classic stereotypes, says Maidenberg. But it’s not just birth order that shapes a little one’s personality. “That's one small component, actually,” she adds. A child’s temperament, genetics, friendships, family dynamics, and even the way parents respond to each child’s needs can all play a role, she says.

For example, older siblings may seem more responsible or organized simply because parents tend to be more cautious and structured with their first child. By the time younger siblings come along, routines loosen, and that can shape behavior too.

From a research perspective, there’s no clear proof that birth order has a lasting impact on personality, addsEileen Kennedy-Moore, Ph.D., a psychologist based in Princeton, New Jersey, and host of the Kids Ask Dr. Friendtastic podcast. “The strongest studies have found no lasting impact of birth order on personality,” she explains.

Still, it can be fun to imagine what your little ones might be like based on the siblings they’ll grow up alongside.

Debunking the birth order stereotypes

Here’s what experts say about where the stereotypes come from, and whether they hold up in real life.

First-born personality traits

The stereotype: Think D.J. Tanner a la Full House: the responsible, caring, and practical one. When asked to describe a typical firstborn, Maidenberg doesn’t hesitate: Perfectionist, driven, responsible, competitive, bossy, and ambitious, she says.

Is the stereotype accurate? These traits can come from how parents approach their first child, says Maidenberg. “There's hypervigilance, so they tend to be more well-behaved. They get more of the positive reinforcement,” she explains. “Since the oldest children often spend more one-on-one time with adults before siblings arrive, they may naturally take on a more authoritative role at home.”

Middle child personality traits

The stereotype: Jan from The Brady Bunch is the classic “middle child” trying to be heard. Middle children are sandwiched between the oldest and youngest, so they tend to be more independent and agreeable, says Maidenberg. Think: peacekeepers and negotiators.

Is the stereotype accurate? “We suspect that when people grow up with moresiblings, there’s a greater need to cooperate, which might then lead people to develop a slightly more cooperative personality,” Lee explains. In fact, a study published inProceedings of the National Academy of Sciencesfound that kids from larger families tended to score a little higher on traits linked to kindness and cooperation (being fair, following rules, and not feeling entitled) and agreeableness (forgiving, patient, and willing to compromise).

Still, statistically speaking, someone from a big family has only about a 60% chance of being a more cooperative person compared to an only child. That’s barely higher than a coin toss.

Youngest child personality traits

The stereotype: Anna from Frozen is the template: the spontaneous, fun-loving one who keeps things light.Youngest childrenare often pegged as outgoing, social, risk-taking, and maybe a little spoiled. “They’re more of a freethinker,” says Maidenberg.

Is the stereotype accurate? Not really. There’s no scientific evidence that being the youngest automatically makes kids more carefree or rebellious. If the shoe fits, it’s probably less about birth order and more about how parents evolve by the time the last baby arrives. “When you get to your last, the vigilance dissipates,” says Maidenberg. “Which goes hand in hand with why they're free thinkers, why they're curious, and why they push the boundaries.”

Only child personality traits

The stereotype: Rory Gilmore is the perfect example: wise beyond their years, articulate, and a little perfectionistic. Only children are often pegged as mature, detail-oriented, confident, and creative, says Maidenberg. And sometimes, they’re labeled as self-centered or spoiled.

Is the stereotype accurate? Nope. Research shows that only children aren’t any more narcissistic than people who grow up with siblings. A 2020 study published inPersonality and Individual Differencescompared adults with and without siblings and found no meaningful differences in narcissism or empathy.

So, where does the “spoiled only” trope come from? “The parents are going to be different parents to an only child than if you have multiple children,” says Maidenberg. “Everything is centered on them. They're the focus of attention at all times.”

But that spotlight doesn’t necessarily shape personality in predictable ways. As Kennedy-Moore explains, “Family dynamics influence kids’ personality, of course. But that’s so individual. It’s not a fixed pattern.”

How to foster individuality in your little one

Parents can focus on seeing each child’s individuality rather than fitting them into birth-order boxes. Noticing and naming what makes your little one unique will help them feel confident in who they are. Here’s how:

Avoid comparing your children

Try your best not to compare their children. “We do it in our heads,” says Kennedy-Moore. “But don't say it aloud to the kids, because we want to give them the opportunity to grow and change,” no matter where they fall in the family birth order. When we openly compare them, we set them into their own stereotype based on their birth order.

Assign tasks to boost self-confidence

“To make sure everychild feels capable, dole out developmentally appropriate tasks,” says Maidenberg. If one child is old enough to grab a diaper, ask them to help. A younger sibling might hold a blanket for the baby, and that small “job” still builds confidence. “They could still get the nuances, that reinforcement that you're giving to the older child,” she says.

Challenge fixed roles

If you notice a child getting stuck in a label, help them see that no one trait defines them. “You don't have to limit some things as being the siblings' turf,” she explains. You might say, “You like dance, and he likes soccer, but we all like to be physically active,” or, “You’re quieter and your brother’s louder, but you both have great things to say.”

Whether your little one is first, middle, last, or only, they’re growing into their own one-of-a-kind story. And you’re guiding them with love.
