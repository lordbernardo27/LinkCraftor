What is precipitous labor? Read one mom's dramatic tale of when she went into labor at home — and how her husband helped deliver their baby.

Every birth story is unique. In our series, “My Birth Story,” we’ve asked moms from all over the world to share their experiences of how they welcomed their little ones into the world. Here, you'll find a range of stories, from moms who delivered vaginally or via C-section, alone or surrounded by family, even some moms who gave birth in under an hour. Their perspectives may all be different — but each one powerfully illustrates the emotion and beauty of giving birth.

January 29, 2014, was the day I saw my husband turn white. I'm talking white-white, like the hue of paper or a fluffy cloud on a pristine summer day.

I was39 weeks pregnantwith our eldest daughter. It was our first child together, but my second baby. I thought I knew what to expect when it came to childbirth. I spent 10 hours bored in a hospital bed when I gave birth to my older son, alerted to the start of labor by a ruptured amniotic sac. On the morning of January 29, when I feltmy first contractionswell across my abdomen, I knew what it was. But I figured I would have some time before the next one. My husband was at an appointment. I was alone. I grit my teeth against the pain, breathing and clenching and waiting for it to subside. Then I relaxed. My husband would be home soon, I thought. It was the first contraction. I had time. Imagine my surprise when the next pain surged across my body just a few minutes later. This time, I picked up the phone.

Precipitous laboris defined as a rapid labor that commences within three hours. Hollywood would have you believe it's far more common than it is; precipitous labor seems to be the way women in TV and the movies quite frequently give birth. Veteran moms like to scoff at the women on the silver screen, writhing and cussing on lawns or in cars or elevators, squeezing out whole human babies in just minutes. They're not wrong. Reporting procedures for this kind of delivery in the U.S. make it difficult to determine an exact figure, butthe CDC puts it at around 2 percent.It’s more likely to occur with teenagers, women with hyptertensive disorders, and women who have had another child. I only met one of those criteria. I figured I was more likely to experience hours of irregular contractions followed by a few more hours of regular contractions and pushing — which is why neither I nor my husband predicted the rapid, Hollywood-style scene that took place in my grandmother-in-law's guest bedroom.

My husband definitely broke some traffic laws. There's no other way he could have gotten home as fast as he did after I called to tell him I was in labor. Quick as he was, by the time he arrived, my contractions were near-constant. The breaks between them were shorter than the contractions themselves. He begged me to go to the car, but instead I ran to the toilet. Something was bearing down inside of me, and I couldn't tell if it was my daughter or a bowel movement. My uterus squeezed while I sat on the toilet. I gripped the edges of the seat, waited for it to pass, and ran back to the bed. Anxiety pulsed through me. I felt electric, like a live wire. My husband watched, speechless, while I ran circles on my hands and knees across the surface of the bed, like some kind of rabid animal. It would have been hilarious, if I wasn’t so terrified. I had no idea what to do.

"Should I call an ambulance?" My husband stood next to the bed, jaw hanging, holding up his cell phone like the liberty beacon.

"No, no, no," I insisted. "I'll go to the car, just wait." Anothercontractionsent me doubling over on top of the bed.

"Okay, let's go," my husband said when my body relaxed. Before I could stand, my body tensed again. I leaned over the edge of the bed, and puked into my husband's empty shoe.

An uncanny calm overtook me. I finally accepted I wasn't leaving that room without a baby in my arms.

"Call the ambulance," I told my husband.

A 911 operator directed my husband to help me lie back. She asked him whether I could wait. I could not. That drive to push is old and animal and it lives deep inside a woman's DNA. When your body tells you it's time, you push.

This is the part where my husband turns white.

I imagine the emergency operator was telling him the paramedics were on their way. I imagine she was coaching him through what to do next. But as he looked down at something I could feel in all its gruesome, unmedicated glory — but could not see — the color literally drained from his face. I watched him hang onto consciousness. There was a moment — a brief one, but in these situations, time dilates — when I thought my husband was going to pass out, and I was going to be all alone. But he didn't pass out, an effort he says he managed through "sheer strength of will." Then someone knocked on the door.

My husband bolted. I heard a crash, which I later learned was my husband knocking over a sidetable full of family photos as he skidded across the floor to the answer the door. Within a few moments, the room flooded with men. Young, strange men in white uniforms — and one woman. For the next twenty minutes, she became the only person in my world.

At first, the paramedic lieutenant tried to cajole me onto a stretcher. But the pain was near constant, and severe, and the baby was already crowning. So she directed my husband to sit behind me on the bed and hold my legs up near my shoulders. I don't really remember screeching while I pushed, but my husband says the sound is burned into his memory.

The feeling of relief once the baby emerges is like nothing else in this world. It is more welcome, more gratifying, and more pleasurable than any other sensation I've experienced. It's like being lifted above the world and out of your pain. But it doesn't last long, because then there is that wail of life bringing you back to your aching and blood-soaked body. And you want to be there, despite the pain, because that body is where your new baby is now being placed.

She was a plump, purplish baby girl and reminded me of a jelly bean. My husband cut the cord in our bedroom. The paramedics helped us down the elevator, and I held my daughter in the back of the ambulance while we rode to the hospital. When we arrived, the entire labor and delivery floor burst into applause. But I could barely hear it, because I was still shocked that I was holding my daughter in my arms instead of my womb.

The entire thing had taken less than two hours.
