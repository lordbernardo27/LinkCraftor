A Doctor's Note on Fertility Trackers

“Fertility awareness is important, and apps and tools are a positive thing,” says Dr. Levine. “But remember: They’re just guides. The best assessment is seeing a fertility doctor and having anultrasoundand blood work.”

According to Dr. Jain, beginners should start with BBT thermometers or ovulation predictor kits to first understand their cycles. For those in need of more detailed data, he shares, “Wearables can be a good investment, especially if you prefer not to manually track symptoms.” Both, he adds, can benefit those with irregular cycle patterns.

However, there are a number of factors that can influence accuracy. “Consistency is key for accurate readings, especially with BBT and wearable devices. Track at the same time each day and in similar conditions,” Dr. Jain explains. “Health factors like stress, illness, medication, sleep patterns, and lifestyle habits can impact readings.”

Types of Fertility Monitors

Some women can track their fertile window by simply analyzing changes in theircervical mucusor charting their basal body temperature. But many benefit from analyzing other forms of biometric data via a fertility tracker, notesDr. Jain. Below, I've outlined the main types of fertility trackers based on expert insights:

  • Hormone Monitors: These fertility monitors calculate hormone levels on a digital device and aim to identify your fertile window and ovulation date. These devices typically collect a urine sample, which calculates hormone levels like estrogen, luteinizing hormone (LH), and progesterone. Some hormone monitors link with an app to chart your data.
  • Thermometers: During the menstrual cycle, your body’s temperature at rest — or basal body temperature (BBT) — rises after ovulation. By tracking your temperature, you can confirm that ovulation has occurred and get a sense of when your ovulation typically occurs each month if your cycles are fairly regular. Some thermometers link with an app to help you track your cycle. However, Dr. Jain warns that accuracy can easily be thwarted by illness, sleep disturbances, alcohol, or inconsistent measurement times.
  • Wearables: Wearable fertility monitors are designed to catch your body’s lowest temperature, typically overnight, and are worn on your wrist, arm or fingers. Some fertility monitors collect additional data, like heart rate and sleep patterns. Another type of wearable fertility monitor goes in the vagina and assesses changes in cervical mucus texture and consistency that indicate fertility.
  • Test Strips: A low-tech but trusted way to track your own fertility is with ovulation test strips or progesterone test strips. These test for LH, which peaks 24 to 48 hours before ovulation occurs, or progesterone, which rises after ovulation to indicate ovulation has occurred. You have to read and track changes in the strips yourself, but there are apps to help! There’s no smart functionality here (you simply urinate on them), but it’s a relatively affordable and a basic, yet effective option.
  • Saliva Monitors: Saliva can change in consistency during fertile windows due to estrogen. These are typically reusable and non-invasive, but can be tricky to interpret correctly, Dr. Jain points out.

Fertility Trackers FAQs

What are fertility trackers?

Fertility trackers are devices that help you to identify your fertile window and ovulation date. If you are trying to conceive, fertility trackers can help you identify the best days to try to get pregnant. Fertility trackers can also help you understand your cycle and fertility.

How accurate are fertility trackers?

It is hard to assess the accuracy of fertility trackers as a whole, as they all function differently, but most devices have been proven to increase your chances of getting pregnant. Depending on your specific body, certain fertility trackers may be better than others for accurately predicting ovulation.

“The most fertile period is the five days leading up to ovulation and the day of ovulation itself,” Dr. Jain says.

How do fertility trackers work?

Fertility trackers work in three main ways: tracking hormone level changes, tracking body temperature changes and tracking cervical mucus changes. Fertility trackers that detect hormone levels typically track the rise of estrogen and/or LH, which indicates ovulation is coming. Temperature trackers look for a rise of BBT above baseline, which occurs after ovulation as progesterone rises. Cervical trackers look for changes in cervical fluid that occur during the fertile window.

How long do I need to track before expecting results and when should I consider seeing a fertility specialist?

For those under the age of 35, Dr. Jain recommends seeing a specialist after 12 months of trying. Those who are older should consider seeing a specialist after 6 months, but adds, “Immediate evaluation is recommended if there are known fertility issues (e.g., irregular periods, endometriosis, history of pelvic infections).”
