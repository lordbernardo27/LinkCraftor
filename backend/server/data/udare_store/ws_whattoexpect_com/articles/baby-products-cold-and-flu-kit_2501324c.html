Quick Summary

Experts recommend stocking your medicine cabinet with sick baby essentials like athermometer,baby-safe chest rub, afever reducer, and anasal aspiratorahead of cold and flu season.

The first time my oldest son caught a cold was rough. He was barely four months old, and I was convinced his snotty nose was the start of something serious. I had athermometer, but no batteries, and my “sick kit” basically amounted to a sample pack ofBoogie Wipesfrom my baby shower.

A reassuring chat with our pediatrician helped ease my worries, but she kindly suggested that I stock up on some essentials for when (not if ) this happened again, especially since we were in the thick of cold and flu season.

Fast forward more than a decade and two kids later, and I can honestly say the anxiety that hits when your baby’s congested never fully goes away (I still check to make sure my preteens are breathing at night when they’re stuffed up). However, I’ve learned that having the right tools on hand — beyond a prescription from the doctor! — makes all the difference. Consider this roundup your cold and flu survival kit: it includes all of the sick day essentials I’ve leaned on over the years to get my family through countless fevers, coughs, and late nights.

A Doctor’s Note

I spoke withBiljana Uzelac, M.D., a board-certified pediatrician with more than 20 years of experience and additional training in integrative medicine, about what families should know before stocking their medicine cabinet. Her top recommendations? Have acool-mist humidifier, anasal aspirator, and asaline sprayready to go.

She says most mild cold symptoms “can be managed at home with rest, fluids, and comfort measures.” For example, try dressing the baby in lightweight clothing, giving extra snuggles and skin-to-skin contact, and slightly elevating the crib mattress (by placing a rolled towel under it, never inside).

How I Chose What To Put in a Cold and Flu Kit

When putting together this survival kit, I leaned on three key factors:

  • Expert insights: I consulted pediatric advice from both theAmerican Academy of Pediatrics(AAP) and my interview with Dr. Uzelac, who walked me through what she recommends all families keep stocked at home.
  • Parent-tested picks: I pulled from my own trial and error after 13 cold and flu seasons, while also tapping into recommendations from the What to Expect Community to see what other parents swear by when their babies are sick.
  • Selection criteria: Everything included is safe for infants and approved by pediatricians, while also being easy to use and widely available (because when you need it, you can’t wait days for shipping).

In other words, these are the tools I wish I’d had on hand the first time my baby spiked a fever — and the ones I’ll never go without again.

Cold and Flu Kit FAQs

What can I give my baby if they have a fever?

Always check with your doctor before giving your baby any fever-reducing medication. The AAP says infant acetaminophen can help reduce a fever (WTE parents preferInfants' Tylenol), and ibuprofen may be used for babies over 6 months. You can also apply a cool compress or give a lukewarm bath to help bring their temperature down.

Are there any essential oils that are good for a sick baby?

Check with your pediatrician first. Using essential oils directly on babies under 2 is generally not recommended — their skin and airways are too sensitive, and oils can cause irritation or breathing issues if misused. However, some baby-safe products (like the chest rub above) use diluted essential oils to aid congestion or sleep.

When should I call a doctor?

Most colds clear up within 7 to 10 days, according to Dr. Uzelac, but you should call your pediatrician if your baby has a fever that lasts longer than three days, a fever above 102°F in older infants and children, or any signs of dehydration (fewer wet diapers, dark urine, no tears when crying).

For babies under 2 months, any temperature of 100.4°F or higher is an emergency and requires an ER visit . Other red flags include trouble breathing, wheezing, persistent ear or sinus pain, bluish lips or face, seizures, or behavior that feels “off.”

“You know your child best,” Dr. Uzelac adds, “so if something is out of the ordinary, seek help.”
