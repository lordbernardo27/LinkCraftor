Help Me, Heidi! My 2-Month-Old Wants Nothing to Do With His Dad

What to do when Baby prefers one parent over another

"My son is almost 2 months old, but he wants nothing to do with his daddy. He’s mama’s boy for sure. Is there anything we can do?" ~Katie

Part of me wants to say: Enjoy your mama’s-only boy while you can. After all, there will come a day (and trust me, that day that will come far sooner than you can imagine right now!) when you’ll wish he'd still fit in your lap…or be willing to sit there for more than a moment. But the other part of me knows how hard a baby-imposed mommy monopoly can be on both you and on Dad — you because you never get a break, and Dad because he feels unloved by the baby he waited so long to love on.

It’s a scenario that plays out with almost all newborns — especially withbreastfed ones, but even with those who formula feed right from the start. Given a choice, choosy babies choose Mom.

And when you consider the initial biological edge brand-new moms have over brand-new dads, it’s no surprise. It’syour voice Baby knows best— having heard it far more clearly than any other sound during his uterine stay (Dad’s voice was muffled, yours was amplified). It’s your scent that Baby is most familiar with, and when it comes to the smell of your breast milk, most attracted to. Studies show that, from the womb, babies respond to their own mom’s voice differently than they do to other voices — their heartbeats accelerate, showing they’re more attentive, more alert, more in tune with the sound of Mom. And this is nothing to sniff at, either: Newborns can pick out their own mom’s breast milk from another mom’s with just a whiff. That familiarity (I know you!) brings a new bundle comfort in a strange new world. A preference for Mom at birth is also, most likely, designed to secure a baby’s survival — after all, as amazing as dads are, they don’t come with a built-in meal ticket…make that, two meal tickets.

Clearly, this Team Mom advantage is biology at work. What it isn’t: a reflection on Dad's parenting skills or his nurturing know-how any more than it is proof of a mom’s innate parenting prowess. The reality is that moms don’t have an exclusive on instincts or intuition. They don’t produce a special nurturing sauce that dads don’t (that is, besides breast milk). My husband, Erik, came on the parenting job with precisely the same experience as I had (zero), and ended up quickly showing more natural ability for caring for our first baby, Emma, than I did.

Sure, it’s hard convincing your husband of that now, especially when he feels like he’s on the outside looking in on your cozy twosome with Baby. But here’s what I know and believe (and what he needs to know and believe): There is nothing that a mom can do that a dad can’t do just as well, if not better, given the chance.

But — and it’s a big but — they have to be given the chance. Sometimes a dad doesn’t give himself the chance — he’s too intimidated bybaby’s natural preference for momand the benefits she comes with (those breasts, that most familiar scent, that most familiar voice).

Sometimes, moms shoo dads to the sidelines without even realizing it, monopolizing baby care (or even becoming hypercritical of a dad’s baby care efforts). That can make dad feel less-than-mom, which, in turn, makes him feel less confident and more tentative with baby — which even a newborn, with his eerily keen instincts, can easily pick up on. Now Dad’s feeling it, Baby’s feeling it, and a cycle has begun that prevents a father from developing his own parenting mojo — and ultimately keeps the two of them from making their own special love connection.

Either way, the truth is that as long as you’re in the room — really, in the house at all — it’ll be hard for them to make that connection. So leave them alone together for increasingly longer periods of time and let them do their thing — no moms allowed. When you’re together, make a conscious effort not to hover when he takes his turns with Baby — or to baby hog (as hard as it is to step away from your newborn). Also encourage them to do lots of skin-to-skin snuggling. Dads, like moms, releaseoxytocin(that bonding hormone) when they cuddle their newborns close. Another great way to boost that bond: baby-wearing. Ifyour sling or carrierdoesn’t fit your husband, consider investing in one that does.

In time, with patience, your mama’s boy will definitely be a daddy’s boy, too. In the meantime, remind him (and yourself!) that while little ones often pick favorites, today’s favorite isn’t always tomorrow’s. He may switch to Team Daddy in the months and years to come, then switch back to Team Mom, and then switch back again. It’s how they roll — and as parents, we just have to learn to roll with it!
