Contact your health care provider as soon as possible if you experience:

  • Very heavy bleeding, like soaking through more than one pad in an hour or noticing tissue or large blood clots that are bigger than an egg
  • A red or swollen leg that feels warm or painful when you touch it
  • Extreme swelling in your hands or face
  • A bad headache that doesn’t get better after taking medication, that affects your vision, that throbs and feels one-sided above your ear, or that starts suddenly with severe pain
  • Changes in your vision, such as blurry vision, blind spots, or flashes of light
  • Dizziness or fainting, either ongoing or that comes and goes
  • A fever of 100.4 Fahrenheit or higher
  • An incision that isn’t healing
  • Severe nausea or vomiting

Call 911 immediately if you experience:

  • Chest pain or a fast heartbeat
  • Trouble breathing or shortness of breath
  • Seizures
  • Thoughts about harming yourself or your baby

Most common postpartum complications

It’s important to reach out to your doctor if you notice any of the symptoms above because all can be signs of a postpartum health problem that may be life-threatening. Three of the most common postpartum complications to be aware of are postpartum heart conditions, postpartum hemorrhage, or deep vein thrombosis (DVT).

Heart problems

Maternal heart disease and related conditions are considered a leading cause of death in pregnant women and new moms, according to the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG). Trusted SourceAmerican College of Obstetricians and GynecologistsPregnancy and Heart DiseaseSee All Sources [4] These conditions can include peripartum cardiomyopathy (PPCM), a rare form of heart failure that appears in the first five to six months after delivery or during the last month of pregnancy, as well as postpartum preeclampsia, high blood pressure that crops up after you’ve had your baby.

Trusted SourceAmerican College of Obstetricians and GynecologistsPregnancy and Heart DiseaseSee All Sources

“I ended up getting postpartum preeclampsia about two days after being home,” says What to Expect Community user Britt5757. “My symptoms were high blood pressure and a headache that would not go away.”

You may be more likely to develop a postpartum heart condition if you have certain risk factors, such as being over 30, being non-Hispanic Black, being pregnant with twins or multiples, having had high blood pressure before or during pregnancy, havinggestational diabetesorobstructive sleep apnea, being obese, or having a history of smoking or maternal cocaine abuse. But other times, these health problems can develop even if you have no risk factors at all.

Postpartum hemorrhage

It’s normal to experience postpartum vaginal bleeding. Lochia is typically dark red in color for up to 10 days after giving birth. After that, it transitions into lighter bleeding or spotting that can last for a few weeks.

But heavier bleeding that doesn’t slow or stop, as well as passing large clots bigger than an egg, may signal that you’re experiencing postpartum hemorrhage. This rare but serious condition affects about 1 in 100 new moms, Trusted SourceStanford MedicinePostpartum HemorrhageSee All Sources [5] and in addition to heavy bleeding, may also be accompanied by signs of a blood pressure drop (blurry vision, chills, fast heartbeat), nausea, and pale skin. “I hemorrhaged after delivering my first, requiring emergency surgery and transfusions and a longer hospital stay,” says What to Expect Community user snock91. “It was really scary.”

New moms may be more likely to hemorrhage after giving birth if they’ve experienced prior pregnancy complications (like uterine inversion, uterine rupture, hydramnios, placenta previa, or placental abruption), have a retained placenta, an unrepaired vaginal or cervical laceration, a bleeding disorder such as Von Willebrand’s Syndrome (which occurs when blood doesn’t clot as it should), or have taken drugs or herbs that interfere with blood clotting, like aspirin, ibuprofen, ginkgo biloba, or large doses of vitamin E.

But, again, it’s also very possible to experience this complication if you don’t have any of these risk factors.

Deep vein thrombosis (DVT)

Deep vein thrombosis (or DVT for short) is a type of blood clot that can lead to a serious condition known as pulmonary embolism (PE). Trusted SourceNational Institutes of Health, MedlinePlusDeep Vein ThrombosisSee All Sources [7] These conditions are more common in pregnant women and new moms, though still rare overall — they occur in about 2 in every 1,000 pregnancies. You may experience tenderness, warmth, or a heavy feeling in your leg (like a bad pulled muscle), or chest pain and unexplained shortness of breath if the blood clot has moved to your lungs.

Trusted SourceNational Institutes of Health, MedlinePlusDeep Vein ThrombosisSee All Sources

“I developed a fairly sizable blood clot (DVT) from my pelvis down to the back of my knee, at about 6 to 7 weeks postpartum,” recalls What to Expect Community user Sarahchis.

Some DVT risk factors include having a family or personal history of venous thromboembolism or severevaricose veins; having preeclampsia, high blood pressure, inflammatory bowel disease, or other vascular diseases; or having postpartum hemorrhage or a blood transfusion.

New moms may also be more likely to develop DVT if they’re older than 35, have had a C-section, were taking birth control pills, or were on strict bed rest. Other new moms can have DVT without any of these risk factors, however.

Postpartum checkups: When they are and how your OB can help spot red flags

Attending all of your postpartum checkups is an important part of taking care of yourself after you’ve given birth. According to ACOG’s latest postpartum care guidelines, women should see their doctors for their first postpartum checkup within three weeks of giving birth and undergo a comprehensive exam within 12 weeks postpartum. Of course, these recommendations are for routine visits. ACOG also says that women and their doctors should have additional check-ins as needed.

Your doctor might suggest coming in sooner or more frequently if you have a specific health risk. And, of course, you should reach out to your practitioner right away if you have any of the red-flag symptoms above, as well as if you have any other concerns or if something simply doesn’t feel right.

Unfortunately, there’s no surefire way to avoid postpartum complications. But there are steps you can take to minimize your risk and protect your health, including:

  • Be proactive. Discuss your health history with your OB/GYN during your pregnancy and come up with a plan for managing any potential problems that may arise based on your risk factors.
  • Stay on top of postpartum visits. Make your three-week and 12-week postpartum checkups a priority and be sure to schedule and attend any other appointments your doctor recommends.

Stay in touch with your doctor. If you notice any unusual postpartum symptoms, speak up! Your health could depend on it.

Postpartum Complications FAQs

When do most postpartum complications happen?

Postpartum complications can happen at any time, including up to a year or more after childbirth, but the first six weeks following birth are a major recovery period. During this time, it’s normal to feel exhausted or experience some pain and bleeding, but you should contact your doctor right away if you have any concerning symptoms.

What postpartum symptoms should not be ignored?

If you experience very heavy bleeding, a bad headache, a red or swollen leg, severe swelling in your hands or face, severe nausea, a fever, or an incision that isn't healing, contact your doctor as soon as possible. If you have chest pain, trouble breathing or a seizure, call 911 immediately.

What are the most serious postpartum complications?

Some serious postpartum complications include postpartum hemorrhage, blood clots (DVT), postpartum preeclampsia, and heart problems, including peripartum cardiomyopathy.

When should I be worried postpartum?

Between postpartum bleeding and sleep deprivation, it's understandable if you're wondering whether your postpartum symptoms are normal. You should seek medical care if you experience warning signs like very heavy bleeding, a bad headache, a red or swollen leg, severe swelling in your hands or face, chest pain, severe nausea, trouble breathing, a seizure, or a fever. It is also perfectly okay to reach out to your medical provider or seek care if you have a gut feeling that something is wrong. And it’s absolutely critical to reach out to your provider right away if you ever have thoughts of harming yourself or your baby.
