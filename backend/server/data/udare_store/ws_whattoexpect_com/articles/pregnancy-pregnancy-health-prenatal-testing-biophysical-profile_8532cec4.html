When and Why is a Biophysical Profile Done?

Updates to copy, information, sources, and formatting

Pregnant woman getting a biophysical profiling done

Find out why your baby might need this special checkup towards the end of pregnancy and how to understand the results.

A biophysical profile (BPP) is typically done in the third trimester to check on your baby's well-being, especially if your pregnancy is considered high-risk, if you're past your due date, or if there are concerns about reduced movement or the amniotic fluid level.

Here’s a closer look at why BPP tests are sometimes performed, when you might get one (or a similar test called a modified biophysical profile), and how to make sense of the results.

Key Takeaways

  • A biophysical profile checks your baby’s well-being. This quick, noninvasive test uses ultrasound (and sometimes fetal heart rate monitoring) to evaluate movement, breathing, muscle tone, and amniotic fluid levels.
  • It’s often used for higher-risk pregnancies. Providers may recommend a BPP in the third trimester if there are concerns including reduced fetal movement, growth issues, you're overdue, or if you have conditions such as high blood pressure or diabetes.
  • Results help guide next steps. Scores range from reassuring (8–10) to needing closer monitoring or even delivery (0–6), helping your provider decide whether it’s safest to continue the pregnancy or intervene.

What is a biophysical profile (BPP)?

The biophysical profile (BPP) is an assessment of your baby's well-being. It can be done on its own, or your health care practitioner may add fetal heart rate monitoring — called a nonstress test — for a more complete picture of your baby's condition. Who is a biophysical profile for?

Your practitioner may order a BPP if they suspect that you or your baby may be at higher risk of complications. The test helps your care team assess whether theplacentaand baby are healthy and it's safe to continue the pregnancy or if it's time to consider delivery.

BPPs are most often done in thethird trimester, usually after32 weeks, but they can be performed any time after24 weeks. They’re typically used for moms who:

  • Are carryingtwins or multiples
  • Have conditions that raise the risk of complications, such ashigh blood pressureordiabetes
  • Have a pregnancy affected byfetal growth restriction
  • Arepast their due date
  • Are experiencing reducedfetal movement
  • Havetoo much amniotic fluidortoo little amniotic fluid
  • Have Rh sensitization

How is a biophysical profile (BPP) done?

A BPP usesultrasoundto look at four specific aspects of your baby's well-being over about 30 minutes:

  • Fetal breathing: Watching your baby's chest movements as she takes practice breaths
  • Fetal movement: Movements of the body or limbs
  • Fetal tone: Flexion and extension of an arm or leg
  • Volume of amniotic fluid: The amount of fluid surrounding your baby

The test may also includefetal heart rate monitoring.

"The BPP was super easy," says Community member lurojackson. "They zoomed in on heart movements, and we even got to see my baby practice breathing!"

What do BPP test results mean?

BPP results reflect several markers, including your baby’sheart rate, breathing (which can includehiccups), movement, muscle tone, and amniotic fluid levels.

Your sonographer will assign a BPP score of 0 points (abnormal) or 2 points (normal) points in each of the four categories listed above and then tally them up. Here’s what the scores mean:

  • 8 to 10 is reassuring and represents a normal, well oxygenated fetus. No intervention is needed, though ongoing testing may be recommended.
  • 4 to 6 may require a second BPP within 24 hours, additional monitoring, or — depending on how far along you are — possible delivery.
  • 0 to 2 typically leads to immediate delivery.

Keep in mind that even if the biophysical profile score is considered "normal," low amniotic fluid levels may require closer monitoring and, in some cases, delivery.

Are there risks of a biophysical profile?

No. BPPs are noninvasive and don't pose any risks to you or your baby.

That said, having the test can understandably make you feel worried or stressed. If you're feeling overwhelmed, chat with your practitioner. You can talk through your concerns together, which can help put your mind at ease. And remember, the test also gives you a chance to see your baby, which can ease anxiety.

"It's reassuring every time I go in and they tell me baby is healthy and we can keep her in there a little longer," says Community mom abadab.

What is the modified biophysical profile?

A modified biophysical profile is anotherprenatal test. It combines the nonstress test with an ultrasound to measure amniotic fluid levels.

While less detailed than a BPP, a modified biophysical profile can be just as useful. Low levels of amniotic fluid may indicate that the baby isn't producing enough urine, for example, and that can point to an issue with the placenta.

If your baby responds normally on the nonstress test and your amniotic fluid levels are adequate, everything is likely progressing well.

Being told you need a biophysical profile can feel worrying. Just remember that the test doesn’t necessarily mean something is wrong. More importantly, the findings help your practitioner make the safest decisions for you and your baby — supporting the healthiest possible pregnancy and delivery.
